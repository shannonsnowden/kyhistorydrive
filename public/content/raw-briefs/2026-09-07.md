🤠 Capt. John Strode of Strode's Station
Virginia militia veteran John Strode reached Boonesborough in 1776 and by 1779 had raised Strode's Station on Strode's Creek—the largest fortified settlement in what became Clark County. Raiders struck in 1781 (two defenders killed) and again later with no fort deaths. The station's burial ground was unearthed in 1965 for a Winchester bypass; remains, including Strode's (d. Aug. 18, 1805), were reinterred in Winchester Cemetery.
https://history.ky.gov/markers/capt-john-strode

🏙️ Nicholasville Takes Shape
After Jessamine County split from Fayette in December 1798, Methodist minister Rev. John Metcalf surveyed a town site on September 16 around four never-failing springs and the Lexington–Danville crossroads. Laid out in 1799 on Caldwell and Gates land and named for Col. George Nicholas (key author of Kentucky's 1792 constitution), it became the county seat that spring and still anchors the Bluegrass south of Lexington.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholasville,_Kentucky

🪶 Piankashaw Claims on the Ohio–Wabash
The Piankashaw (Peeyankihšiaki), a Miami-Illinois people of the lower Wabash, were among the nations that still asserted hunting and occupancy rights along the Ohio when Kentucky entered the Union. At Vincennes on August 27, 1804, they ceded to the United States the tract between the Ohio and Wabash rivers below Clark's Grant—the corridor facing western Kentucky toward the Falls of the Ohio.
https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-piankeshaw-1804-0072

🏛️ Wright Mounds, Montgomery County
Three conical Adena mounds (15Mm6–8) once crowned a Bluegrass ridgetop. University of Kentucky crews excavated two in 1937: the larger stood about 30 feet high and 180 feet across, raised in phases over circular paired-post ritual screens, with 21 burials (14 in log tombs) and deliberately broken feast pottery between layers. The smaller mound, built in one phase over a similar screen, held only two people.
https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Wright-Mounds.aspx

🕳️ Diamond Caverns' Decorated Limestone
Discovered July 14, 1859, near Park City and opened as a show cave within a month, Diamond Caverns cuts Mississippian Ste. Genevieve limestone in the same karst country as Mammoth Cave. Without Mammoth's Big Clifty sandstone caprock, surface water keeps dissolving and redepositing calcite—so the passages glitter with stalactites, flowstone, and "diamond" crystals that gave the cave its name.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Caverns
