🪶 Cornstalk (Hokoleskwa), from Point Pleasant to Fort Randolph
Hokoleskwa, “a blade of corn,” called Cornstalk by the colonists, was a leader of the Mekoche division of the Shawnee and their main war chief in Lord Dunmore’s War. That war grew out of the 1768 Treaty of Fort Stanwix, in which the Iroquois ceded land south of the Ohio River, present West Virginia and Kentucky, to the British without consulting the Shawnees who hunted there. On October 10, 1774, 252 years ago today, Cornstalk struck Col. Andrew Lewis’s wing of the Virginia army at Point Pleasant, where the Kanawha meets the Ohio, before it could unite with Lord Dunmore’s. Through the day-long battle Virginians heard him urging his men to “be strong, be strong!” The Shawnees had the upper hand at first, but colonial reinforcements arrived, and at nightfall the outnumbered warriors quietly withdrew back across the Ohio. At Camp Charlotte, Cornstalk then apparently accepted the Ohio River as the boundary of Shawnee lands, the peace that opened Kentucky to the settlers of 1775 (that treaty has its own story here), and he gave four hostages, including his son. A Virginia officer wrote that he had never heard an orator surpass him. When the Revolution began, Cornstalk argued for neutrality, fearing that war with the Americans would be disastrous for the Shawnees, while militant bands under leaders such as Blackfish went their own way. In the fall of 1777 he went to Fort Randolph, built on the Point Pleasant battlefield, to learn American intentions, and the commander, Matthew Arbuckle, detained him with his son Elinipsico and two other Shawnees. On November 10, after unknown Indians killed a militiaman nearby, soldiers killed all four prisoners. More Shawnees joined the militants after the murder, though historian John Sugden argues it did not by itself bring the Shawnees into the war. The accused killers were tried and acquitted. Point Pleasant is in West Virginia, so this story has no map pin.

🤠 Esther Whitley: over the Cumberlands in 1775
Esther Gill Fullen (1755–1833) was born in Augusta County, Virginia, and married William Whitley there; together they had eight daughters and three sons. In November 1775 she started west along the Wilderness Road with her husband and their family, and she is remembered as the third woman of European descent to cross the Cumberland Mountains. The Whitleys made their home near Crab Orchard, in what is now Lincoln County, on an estate called Sportsman’s Hill. Its brick house is described as the first in Kentucky, and beside it ran an oval track where horses raced counterclockwise, the opposite of British racing. Esther died at the home of her daughter Ann Harper in Woodford County on November 20, 1833. The house survives as the William Whitley House State Historic Site, which has its own story here; the map pin is that house.

⛰️ Pine Mountain and Pound Gap
Pine Mountain is a single ridge of the Appalachians running about 125 miles from near Pioneer, Tennessee, to near Elkhorn City, Kentucky. Its highest point, Birch Knob, rises 3,273 feet on the Kentucky–Virginia border. It has long been a barrier to travel: only two waterways pass through the whole ridge, the Cumberland River at Pineville and the Clear Fork near Jellico, Tennessee. Travelers crossed at gaps instead, and the best known on the Virginia line is Pound Gap, between Jenkins, Kentucky, and Pound, Virginia. Ohio Company surveyors, possibly including Christopher Gist, passed through it in 1750, and the Kentucky Historical Society’s marker credits Gist with finding the route on April 1, 1751. Hunters used the gap over the following decade. In 1774 Daniel Boone, who called it “Sounding Gap,” crossed here with Michael Stoner to warn surveyors of a possible Shawnee attack. Around 1798 seven families, the Adamses, Webbs, Caudills, Crafts, Hammonds, Sturgills, and Collinses, came through to settle what became Letcher County, and in 1834 the General Assembly acted to improve the road from Mount Sterling to Pound Gap, a route used to drive horses, hogs, and cattle to Virginia and southern markets. War came through too. On March 16, 1862, Union troops under Brig. Gen. James A. Garfield forced about 500 Confederates from the gap, and on June 1, 1864, on their last raid into Kentucky, Morgan’s Raiders dislodged Union forces here. U.S. 23 now runs through a cut at the top. The map pin is an approximate point on the Kentucky side of the gap, near Jenkins.

⛪ Old Mulkey Meetinghouse, 1804
South of Tompkinsville in Monroe County, off KY 1446, stands a log meetinghouse built in 1804 for the Mill Creek Baptist Church, a congregation of pioneer Baptists from the Carolinas led by John and Philip Mulkey. A religious revival swept Kentucky in the early 1800s, and by 1803 the congregation had grown to 125 members. The church minutes of May 11, 1804, called for a meetinghouse 50 feet long and 30 feet wide, with jointed wood shingles, five windows, and three doors, its builder to be paid in trade by subscription. The finished building, of half-hewn logs chinked with rocks and mud, is shaped like a cross with twelve corners, said to stand for the twelve apostles or the twelve tribes of Israel, and its three doors are said to symbolize the Trinity. It had no fireplace. Worshippers warmed their feet in bags of heated stones, and the men took turns tending an outdoor fire and keeping guard. John Mulkey is said to have preached more than 10,000 sermons, and his descendants helped establish more than 50 Disciples of Christ congregations. Services were held there until 1856. The cemetery holds Revolutionary War soldiers and pioneers, among them Daniel Boone’s sister Hannah Boone Stewart Pennington, who died in 1828. The site became Kentucky’s 19th state park in 1931. It is often called the oldest log meetinghouse in Kentucky, though the state’s nature preserves office calls it the second-oldest church meetinghouse. The map pin marks the meetinghouse.

🏛️ Liberty Hall: John Brown’s house in the new capital
In 1786 James Wilkinson bought much of the land that is now downtown Frankfort and laid out the town, naming its streets for friends, famous people and places, and himself. Three of his streets, Wilkinson, Wapping, and Montgomery (now Main), bound three sides of the Liberty Hall grounds. In 1796 John Brown (1757–1837) bought the four-acre lot overlooking the Kentucky River. Brown had represented Virginia in the Continental Congress and in the U.S. Congress, where he introduced the bill granting Kentucky statehood, and Kentucky’s legislature then elected him to the U.S. Senate, where he served until 1805. Often away on government business in Philadelphia, he built a two-story, five-bay brick house, one of the earliest brick homes in Frankfort, from bricks fired from clay dug out of its own cellar. It was substantially complete in 1800, and its glass windows were added in 1804. The National Park Service calls it one of the most perfect examples of Federal domestic architecture ever realized in the country, patterned on the finest houses Brown had seen in the East. The grounds also held a kitchen and laundry, a smokehouse, a privy, stables, a carriage house, and slave quarters. In 1835 Brown divided the property so his sons would inherit equally: Mason received Liberty Hall, and for Orlando he hired Gideon Shryock, designer of the Kentucky State Capitol, to design the Greek Revival Orlando Brown House. Liberty Hall opened as a museum in 1937 and was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1971. The map pin marks the house at 218 Wilkinson Street.
