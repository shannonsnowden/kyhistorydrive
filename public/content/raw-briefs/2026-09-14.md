🤠 Hancock Taylor, Fincastle surveyor
Virginia deputy surveyor Hancock Taylor worked under Col. William Preston mapping Kentucky bounty land in the early 1770s. He had already been at the Falls of the Ohio in 1769 en route to New Orleans and was surveying in Kentucky by 1773, helping lay out tracts that later underpinned Louisville and Bluegrass claims. Near the mouth of the Kentucky River in July 1774—as Lord Dunmore’s War flared—he was shot by Native warriors; companions brought him toward the settlements until he died just south of present-day Richmond and was buried on Taylor’s Fork of Silver Creek (Kentucky Historical Society roadside marker).
Source: https://history.ky.gov/markers/grave-of-hancock-taylor

🏙️ Lawrenceburg from Kaufman’s Station
German immigrant Jacob Kaufman planted a frontier outpost about 1780 that locals called Kaufman’s or Coffman’s Station, a few miles west of the Kentucky River in what is now Anderson County. The post office opened as Lawrenceburg in 1817 for tavern keeper William Lawrence; Franklin County’s court incorporated the place as Lawrence in 1820, and in 1827—when Anderson County was cut from Franklin and Mercer—it took the name Lawrenceburg and became the new county seat. That river-edge Bluegrass perch later grew into a bourbon town (Wild Turkey, Four Roses), but the town story starts with Kaufman’s station and Lawrence’s tavern.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Lawrenceburg,_Kentucky

🪶 Wyandot on Kentucky’s northern frontier
The Wyandot (Wendat/Huron descendants), an Iroquoian people whose towns clustered north of the Ohio after Beaver Wars upheaval, still used Kentucky for winter camps and hunts and joined British-allied war parties against the stations. In August 1782 Wyandot warriors fought beside Shawnee, Lenape, and Capt. William Caldwell’s Rangers at the Siege of Bryan Station, then helped spring the ambush at Blue Licks three days later—the fight that killed Col. John Todd, Col. Stephen Trigg, and Israel Boone. Treat them as their own nation in the record, not a footnote to a generic “Indian raid.”
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/590

🏺 Read Shell Midden (15Bt10), Butler County
On the Green River west of Morgantown, the Read Shell Midden is another Late Archaic (roughly 3000–1000 BCE) camp-and-cemetery in the same mussel-shoal world as Indian Knoll, Carlston Annis, and Ward. WPA crews dug it from 1937 to 1939 under William S. Webb; more than 240 burials were identified, though poor preservation left only about 152 individuals removable for study. Webb’s 1950 report and later paleodemography treat Read as seasonal habitation plus mortuary ground. It joined the National Register in 1986 with the Green River Shell Middens thematic group—Kentucky’s densest Archaic shell-midden landscape, still pinned to the river’s Big Bend.
Source: https://npgallery.nps.gov/AssetDetail/NRIS/86000647

🪨 Kentucky River Palisades
For roughly a hundred miles through the Inner Bluegrass, the Kentucky River cuts entrenched meanders into Middle Ordovician High Bridge Group limestone and dolostone—Camp Nelson, Tyrone, and Oregon beds that hold vertical cliffs while softer Lexington Limestone weathers to gentler slopes. Movement on the Kentucky River Fault System helped jack those resistant rocks into the path of downcutting, so the gorge keeps its meander shape from an older, flatter landscape. Fort Boonesborough sat near the eastern end of the palisades; the same cliffs still frame Clays Ferry, Camp Nelson, and the river road from Frankfort toward Boonesboro.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kentucky_River_Palisades
