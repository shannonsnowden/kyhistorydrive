🤠 Benjamin Logan
In 1775 Benjamin Logan left Boone's party and built Logan's Station (St. Asaph) near present-day Stanford — a stockade by Buffalo Spring that held through a roughly thirteen-day siege in May 1777. Logan hauled the wounded Burr Harrison inside under fire, then slipped out at night and walked about 150 miles to the Holston settlements for powder. He later helped write Kentucky's first constitution, then settled on Bullskin Creek near Finchville in Shelby County, where he died in 1802.

🏙️ Shelbyville
Shelby County was carved from Jefferson County in 1792 and named for first governor Isaac Shelby. That October, at the first county court, Revolutionary veteran William Shannon donated about 50 acres on Clear Creek (plus an acre for public buildings) so the new seat would sit on the Frankfort-to-Falls road. Trustees required 1½-story hewed-log houses with brick or stone chimneys; by 1800 the town had 262 people. Nearby Squire Boone's Painted Stone Station (1780) had been the earlier foothold — Shelbyville won the courthouse.

🦴 Falls of the Ohio
About 390 million years ago a shallow Devonian sea laid down the Jeffersonville Limestone — coral banks and stromatoporoid sponges now exposed at the Falls of the Ohio. Those hard shelves made the only major rapids on the river, so flatboats had to portage; Louisville and the Indiana towns grew at that choke point (George Rogers Clark staged from Corn Island in 1778). National Natural Landmark since 1966.

🏹 Chickasaw hunting grounds
West of the Tennessee River, Chickasaw hunting grounds stayed off Kentucky's settled map until October 19, 1818. Andrew Jackson and Isaac Shelby negotiated what became the Jackson Purchase: roughly 13,000 square miles (about 2,369 in Kentucky) for $300,000 in annuities. That treaty filled in the western counties from Ballard through McCracken and closed the last big Indigenous title claim inside the state.

🏇 William Whitley / Sportsman's Hill
Near Crab Orchard, William Whitley finished Sportsman's Hill (1787–1794), often called Kentucky's first brick house — thick walls, elevated windows, and hiding places against raids. Beside it he ran a clay oval where horses raced counter-clockwise, a pointed break from English turf clockwise. The house and hill remain a Lincoln County historic site.
