🤠 Michael Stoner, German long hunter
Born Michael Holsteiner near Philadelphia in 1748, Stoner grew up speaking German and drifted to Virginia’s New River frontier as a teen. In June 1774, Capt. William Russell picked him and Daniel Boone to warn Virginia surveyors scattered across Kentucky during Dunmore’s War. The pair covered roughly 800 miles in about 61–62 days—Harrod’s new town, the Kentucky River, the Falls of the Ohio, then home by Cumberland Gap—pulling most parties back before Shawnee and Mingo strikes closed the country. Stoner later hunted for Boonesborough, took a wound in 1777, and settled in Clark County before ending his days in Wayne County (d. 1814/15).
Source: https://winchestersun.com/2020/07/31/where-in-the-world-who-was-michael-stoner/

🏙️ Taylorsville on Brashears Creek
In 1799, while the forks of Brashears Creek and the Salt River still sat in Shelby County, miller Richard Taylor asked the Shelby County Court to carve about sixty acres from his tract for a town; trustees George Cravinston, William Bridgewater, Robert Jeffries, Elijah Prewitt, and Isaac Ellis laid it out and named it for him. Steamboats could push all the way up the Salt from the Ohio, which helped the site stick. When Spencer County was created in 1824, Taylorsville became the seat and was incorporated in 1829—still a short hop from Bagdad and Shelbyville.
Source: https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=136837

🪶 Delaware (Lenape) in the 1780 Kentucky raids
Pushed west from the Delaware Valley, Lenape bands hunted and wintered south of the Ohio alongside Shawnee and others even after summer towns sat north of the river. The 1778 Treaty of Fort Pitt briefly allied some Delawares with the United States, then collapsed after White Eyes’s death and American attacks on Delaware towns. In summer 1780, Col. Henry Bird’s British–Native column came up the Licking with artillery; Delaware warriors were among the nations that took Ruddle’s and Martin’s stations, dividing captives toward Detroit until exchanges after Greenville (1795).
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/591

🏺 Cleek-McCabe Fort Ancient village
On Mud Lick Creek’s floodplain in Boone County (near Walton), Cleek-McCabe was a thirteenth-century Fort Ancient farming town: a ~100-foot midden ring around a ~200-foot plaza, with burial mounds on the east and west. University of Kentucky crews in the late 1930s dug the western mound and found three successive roofless public structures stacked on the same spot—first a 43-foot circle, then rectangles up to 56 by 95 feet—plus a central ceremonial pole, rock “pavements,” and guilloche-incised jars that tie the site to neighbors in SW Ohio and SE Indiana. The eastern mound still stands.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Cleek-McCabe.aspx

🥃 Jacob Beam on Hardin Creek
After 1790, Johannes Jacob Beam (Boehm) settled a farm and grist mill on Hardins Creek in Washington County (Outer Bluegrass / Knobs, feeding the Rolling Fork). Around 1795 he began selling corn whiskey from surplus grain—the start of the Beam line that later became Jim Beam. The creek itself is named for settler Martin Hardin; a 1789 Presbyterian meeting house nearby seeded Lebanon, and Maryland Catholics later planted St. Charles Church along the same waters.
