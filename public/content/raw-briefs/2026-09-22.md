John Finley, Springfield’s Founding, and Ruddell’s Station

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Tuesday, September 22, 2026*

🤠 John Finley, the Trader Who Put Kentucky on Boone’s Map
Long before Wilderness Road fame, Pennsylvania-born trader John Finley (also Findley) was crossing the Alleghenies for furs. Around 1752 he reached Eskippakithiki, the Shawnee town in today’s Clark County, one of the earliest recorded Euro-American trading trips into central Kentucky. Years later, as a Braddock-campaign acquaintance of Daniel Boone, he returned to the Yadkin with stories of cane-rich hunting grounds beyond Cumberland Gap. In May 1769 he guided Boone’s party through that gap; Finley’s firsthand routes—not Boone alone—helped open the country settlers would soon claim.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Finley_(frontiersman)

🏙 Springfield Founding and Washington County Seat
Washington County was the first county the new Kentucky legislature created after 1792 statehood, named for George Washington. In 1793 a site near the old Wilderness Trace was chosen for the seat on land tied to Gen. Matthew Walton; surveyor David Caldwell laid out the public square, and the town took the name Springfield. Early stations—Sandusky (1776), Cartwright’s (1778)—and Parker’s Landing on Beech Fork had already drawn settlers from the Gap road; Maryland Catholics arrived in force from 1785. The Lincoln family later settled nearby on Beech Fork, and Thomas Lincoln and Nancy Hanks’s marriage bond still sits in the Washington County clerk’s office.
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/665

🏹 Delaware Winter Camps on Kentucky Ground
After the Beaver Wars scattered Algonquian peoples across the Ohio Valley, Lenape (Delaware) bands—often alongside Shawnee, Mingo, Miami, and Wyandot—used Kentucky as seasonal hunting country rather than a place of large permanent towns. From villages north of the Ohio they slipped south in fall and winter for deer and bear, leaving small camps that Anglo hunters mistook for emptiness. By the 1770s those same parties faced settler pressure and increasingly turned from hunts into raids. The “dark and bloody ground” myth grew from that pattern: real Native use, but not the empty wilderness Filson’s readers imagined.
Source: https://preservationkentucky.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Dispelling-the-Myth-17th-18th-Century-Indian-Life-in-KY.pdf

🏛 Peter Village: Early Woodland Enclosure Near Lexington
North of Lexington in Fayette County, Peter Village (15Fa166) is a huge Early Woodland ditch-and-embankment enclosure—about 9.2 hectares, irregular rather than a neat geometric circle. Long mislabeled an Adena “village,” it is better read as a specialized gathering and craft place; archaeologist Berle Clay tied it to barite and galena working, and newer geoarchaeology (Henry and others) points to dual embankments and construction as early as the 5th century BCE—among the earliest monumental enclosures in the Middle Ohio Valley. It sits near the better-known Mount Horeb circle, marking Bluegrass ritual landscapes centuries before forts and stations.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Horeb_Earthworks_Complex

⚔ Isaac Ruddell and the Guns at Ruddell’s Station
In spring 1779 Capt. Isaac Ruddell rebuilt a fort on the South Fork of Licking (Harrison County) on the old Hinkson site—Ruddell’s Station, or Fort Liberty—soon joined by many Pennsylvania German families. In June 1780 British Capt. Henry Bird marched south with cannon, Canadian volunteers, and hundreds of Native allies. Artillery cracked the log walls; Ruddell surrendered under a promise of protection, then watched the agreement collapse as roughly four hundred captives were marched toward Detroit. Two of his sons were adopted by Shawnee; Stephen later lived as Tecumseh’s companion. The British never again brought that scale of artillery into Kentucky.
Source: https://www.battlefields.org/learn/revolutionary-war/battles/ruddells-station
