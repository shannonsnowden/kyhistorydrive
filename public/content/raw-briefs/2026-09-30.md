🤠 Levi Todd, Lexington Co-Founder
Levi Todd (1756–1807) came to Kentucky in 1775 with his brothers John and Robert, helped lay out Lexington’s stockade, and was elected one of its first Gentlemen Trustees in March 1776. He became Kentucky County’s first clerk in 1777, rode with George Rogers Clark’s Illinois campaign (including the capture of Kaskaskia in 1778), and later served decades as Fayette County clerk. In August 1782 he led a forty-man relief column toward Bryan Station, then fought at Blue Licks—where brother Col. John Todd was killed—and wrote one of the earliest official accounts of the defeat. He rose to major general of Kentucky militia, built Ellerslie (Fayette’s first brick house), and was grandfather of Mary Todd Lincoln.

🏙️ New Castle, Henry County Seat
When the General Assembly cut Henry County from Shelby County in 1798 and named it for Virginia’s Patrick Henry, New Castle was fixed as the seat—just north of Shelby County, inside the Louisville–Lexington–Cincinnati triangle. Landowners opened a courthouse square; the town was incorporated in 1817 and is counted among Kentucky’s oldest incorporated communities. Tobacco, hay, and cattle long carried the surrounding farms, and the courthouse square still anchors a preserved downtown with the Caplinger House (1863) as the Henry County History Center.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_County,_Kentucky

🪶 Susquehannock and the Ohio Valley Door
The Susquehannock (also Andaste / Minqua / later Conestoga) were an Iroquoian people of the lower Susquehanna who dominated mid-Atlantic fur trade in the early 1600s and fought the Haudenosaunee through the Beaver Wars. After Iroquois conquest around 1675–76, their power collapsed and survivors scattered or were absorbed. That hinge helped empty and reopen the Ohio Valley as contested “middle ground”—the hunting country Shawnee, Mingo, and others later claimed, and the same corridor Virginia traders and long hunters followed into Kentucky. Their story is not a Kentucky village tale, but one of the eastern Beaver Wars shocks that remade who could hunt south of the Ohio.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Susquehannock

🏛️ Dover Mound, Mason County
On a ridge near Dover in Mason County, the Dover Mound was an Adena (Early Woodland) burial mound. University of Kentucky anthropologists William S. Webb and Charles E. Snow excavated it in the early 1950s and reported fifty-five burials with accompanying artifacts—one of the fuller published Adena mortuary assemblages from northern Kentucky’s Ohio River hills. Their 1959 monograph still anchors what we know of the site and places Dover in the same Adena world as Mount Horeb and other Bluegrass earthworks, centuries before Fort Ancient villages lined the same river.
Source: https://www.kentuckypress.com/9780813155630/the-dover-mound/

🏰 Bryan Station Siege, August 1782
Bryan Station (also Bryan’s / Bryant’s) sat on the south bank of Elkhorn Creek a few miles northeast of Lexington, planted in 1776 by the Bryan brothers and Boone in-laws from North Carolina. On August 15–16, 1782, Capt. William Caldwell, Simon Girty, and several hundred British-allied Wyandot, Lenape, and Shawnee warriors surrounded the fort. Knowing the attackers hoped to ambush men at the spring, the garrison sent the women and girls out with their usual water pails; they filled the fort’s supply and returned unharmed. The siege failed to take the station; when Kentucky militia pursued three days later, they met disaster at Blue Licks.
