🤠 Col. William Russell III
Born in Culpeper County, Virginia, in 1758, William Russell III rode west as a teenager on his father’s 1773 attempt to settle Kentucky—the same trek that ended when James Boone and Henry Russell were killed. He later served at Kings Mountain as an aide to Col. William Campbell, then settled Fayette County in 1783 on his father’s military land. He sat in Kentucky’s early house, led militia in the Northwest Indian War, and as colonel of the 7th U.S. Infantry fought at Fort Harrison and in the Peoria War. Russell County, Kentucky, is named for him (Russellville honors his father).
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Russell_(Kentucky_politician)

🏙 Owensboro / Yellow Banks
Frontiersman William Smeathers (Bill Smothers) built a cabin on the Ohio’s yellow clay banks about 1797–98, and travelers already called the landing Yellow Banks. Van Cleve’s 1794 journal noted three families there; French spy Victor Collot counted eight or ten by 1796. After Daviess County formed in 1815, the town was laid out in 1816 and chartered Owensborough in 1817 for Col. Abraham Owen, killed at Tippecanoe; the spelling shortened to Owensboro in 1893. Smothers Park still marks the pioneer riverfront.

🪶 Tutelo & the Totteroy
The Tutelo (Yesą́), Eastern Siouan kin to the Saponi and Occaneechi, are tied in tradition and early maps to the Big Sandy—called the Totteroy River on mid-1700s British charts along today’s Kentucky–West Virginia line. Iroquois pressure in the later Beaver Wars (~1670s) scattered them toward the Virginia Piedmont; Batts and Fallam met “Totero Town” near Salem in 1671. Mid-18th-century Bluegrass accounts still list Tutelo among peoples using the middle Ohio Valley before large villages south of the river faded after 1758.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Tutelo

🏺 Annis Mound & Village
On the Green River’s Big Bend in Butler County, Annis (15Bt2 / 15Bt20) was a Middle Mississippian mound center of the early–mid 1300s. UK’s WPA crew (1939–40) and Penn State (2002–04) mapped three expanding palisades (outermost ~4.5 acres with a bastion), 17 structures, and a 110-by-110-foot, 12-foot platform mound that shifted from chiefly residence to ritual summit and back. Labor estimates put the first mound stage at about 5.5 days for 50 workers. A 9-acre portion joined the National Register in 1985.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/annis_mound_and_village_site

🪨 Cumberland Falls
Where the Cumberland crosses the hard Lower Pennsylvanian Rockcastle conglomerate, softer rock below erodes faster and keeps a ~68-foot, 125-foot-wide drop—the “Niagara of the South.” The falls likely began near the Pottsville Escarpment by Burnside and retreated about 45 miles upstream, cutting the gorge. On clear full-moon nights the mist throws a moonbow, the only regularly predictable one in the Western Hemisphere. T. Coleman du Pont’s gift helped make it a state park in 1931.
