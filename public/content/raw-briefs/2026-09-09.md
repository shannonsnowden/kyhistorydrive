Callaway, Cynthiana & the Fire Nation

Kentucky History Morning Update — Wednesday, September 9, 2026

🤠 Col. Richard Callaway of Boonesborough
Virginia-born Richard Callaway (1717–1780) helped blaze the path into central Kentucky with Boone in 1775 and co-founded Boonesborough for Henderson’s Transylvania venture. He led the rescue when his daughters and Jemima Boone were taken in 1776, sat as a Kentucky County burgess and militia colonel, and defended the fort through the 1778 siege—then pressed court-martial charges against Boone afterward. In 1780, while building his authorized ferry boat on the Kentucky River, he was ambushed and killed by a Shawnee war party; Calloway County later took his name.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Callaway

🏙️ Cynthiana — Cynthia & Anna on the Licking
Harrison County was carved from Bourbon and Scott in 1793 and named for Col. Benjamin Harrison, a Ruddle’s Station settler and early Kentucky convention man. The same December acts made the county seat a new town on 150 acres Robert Harrison, a blacksmith (no proven kin to Benjamin), donated opposite the mouth of Gray’s Run on the South Fork of the Licking. He asked it be named for his daughters Cynthia and Anna—hence Cynthiana—then moved on to Ohio and Indiana while the square grew into the seat.
Source: https://kentuckyhistorictravels.com/2023/02/03/cynthiana-ky/

🪶 Mascouten — “Fire Nation” on the Wabash
French Jesuits called the Algonquian Mascouten the Fire Nation (a name-play on prairie/fire roots); after losses in the upper lakes they regrouped with Kickapoo and Fox peoples and by the late 1700s lived on the Wabash with Piankashaw and Kickapoo neighbors. That Wabash corridor faced western Kentucky across the Ohio: Mascouten bands sided with the British against Clark’s Illinois campaign, stayed in the western alliance after the Revolution, and only pulled back after Kentucky militia under Col. John Hardin struck Wabash towns in 1791 and took hostages.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascouten

🏺 Howard Site — Late Fort Ancient above Harts Fork
In Madison County, the Howard site (15Ma427 / 15Ma127) holds a Late Fort Ancient farming village (roughly A.D. 1400–1750) on a low ridge above Harts Fork: house clusters, shell-tempered jars with geometric necks, small triangular points, and hide scrapers that outnumber arrowheads. Excavations also turned up mica from the southern Appalachians plus a blue glass bead and a tubular copper bead—signs of long-distance Native exchange and early European trade goods reaching the Kentucky Bluegrass before permanent Anglo settlement.
Source: https://kentuckyarchaeologicalsurvey.org/howard-site/

🥃 Evan Williams on the Louisville wharf
Welsh immigrant Evan Williams (1755–1810) distilled corn whiskey on Louisville’s Ohio bank and by 1801 held a federal license for three stills (141, 130, and 93 gallons), shipping barrels by flatboat; he also served as the town’s first wharf master. Markers and the modern Heaven Hill brand still call him Kentucky’s first commercial distiller (1783), but Filson historian Michael Veach notes that “first” claim bloomed late (1892) and that other settlers were likely running stills by 1779—so the legend outruns the paperwork even if Williams was a real early Louisville whiskey man.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evan_Williams_(bourbon)
