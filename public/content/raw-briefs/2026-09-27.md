# Kentucky History Morning Update — 2026-09-27

This morning's Kentucky history brief:

Worthington's Station, Paducah, and the Travelling Church

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Sunday, September 27, 2026*

🤠 Edward Worthington at Worthington's Fort
Irish-born Edward Worthington (c. 1754–1804) reached the Kentucky country as a surveyor on Beargrass Creek by 1775, fought in Lord Dunmore's War, and was wounded defending McClelland's Station against Mingo warriors under Pluggy on December 29, 1776. As a captain and paymaster under George Rogers Clark he helped take Vincennes (1779), then planted Worthington's Fort about four miles southeast of present Danville. In 1780 Clark ordered his company to build Fort Jefferson on the Chickasaw Bluffs near the Ohio–Mississippi confluence; continuous Chickasaw pressure forced its abandonment in 1781. Worthington later died of yellow fever in New Orleans while chasing a land-fraud suit.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Worthington

🏙️ Paducah at the Tennessee mouth
After the 1818 Jackson Purchase opened Chickasaw land west of the Tennessee River, a small landing called Pekin grew at the Ohio–Tennessee confluence. In 1827 William Clark (of Lewis and Clark, then Superintendent of Indian Affairs) platted a town on that ground and named it Paducah for the Padouca, a people he had known on the plains and believed nearly erased by European expansion—not the later folk legend of a Chickasaw "Chief Paduke." The town incorporated in 1830 and became McCracken County's seat in 1832, riding steamboat traffic into a river-and-rail hub.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paducah,_Kentucky

🏹 Potawatomi fire-keepers and the Ohio Valley wars
The Potawatomi (Bodéwadmi, "keepers of the fire") were the youngest sibling of the Anishinaabe Council of Three Fires. In the Beaver Wars they fought as French-allied Algonquians against Haudenosaunee expansion that emptied much of the mid-Ohio Valley into contested hunting ground. A century later Potawatomi warriors stood in the Northwest Indian Confederacy; Kentucky militia columns under Scott and Wilkinson struck their Miami–Wea neighbors north of the Ohio to blunt raids on Bluegrass stations. The same wars that cleared and recontested Kentucky's "middle ground" pulled Great Lakes fire-keepers into Kentucky's frontier story.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potawatomi

🏛️ Slack Farm (15Un28), Union County
On the Ohio River floodplain in Union County, Slack Farm was a large Caborn-Welborn Mississippian village occupied about 1400–1700 CE. Across roughly 75 acres, residents built at least seven residential clusters with wall-trench wattle-and-daub houses, deep corn-storage pits, and cemeteries oriented toward plazas. Long-distance trade reached north for catlinite and south for Nodena points and marine shell; by about 1550, glass beads and reworked brass show indirect European contact. After severe looting in 1987, Kentucky Heritage Council, University of Kentucky, and Indiana University crews—with hundreds of volunteers—mapped more than 450 looter holes and documented the village layout before reinterring remains on site.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Slack-Farm.aspx

⛪ The Travelling Church, 1781
In fall–winter 1781, Rev. Lewis Craig led roughly 200 Baptists and about 400 other settlers from Spotsylvania County, Virginia—the largest single pioneer party yet to enter the Kentucky District. Capt. William Ellis guided the train through Cumberland Gap; they raised a fortified meetinghouse and Craig's Station at Gilbert's Creek (near present Lancaster) that December. Members later founded Gilbert's Creek Baptist (still active), South Elkhorn, Clear Creek, and other congregations that seeded central Kentucky's Baptist landscape. Lewis's brother Elijah stayed behind in Virginia awhile to press for religious liberty with James Madison before following west.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travelling_Church
