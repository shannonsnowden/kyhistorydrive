🤠 Col. John Todd of Lexington
Pennsylvania-born lawyer John Todd (1750–1782) reached Kentucky around 1775, sat in Virginia’s legislature for Kentucky County, rode with George Rogers Clark to Kaskaskia and Vincennes, and was named civil commandant of Illinois County. Back home he became Fayette’s first militia colonel (Boone as lieutenant colonel) and a Lexington trustee who deeded land to the young town. After Bryan Station’s siege he led the pursuit that ended at Blue Licks on August 19, 1782, where he was killed at 32; brother Levi helped bury the dead. Todd County (1819) still carries his name—great-uncle of Mary Todd Lincoln.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Todd_County,_Kentucky

🏙 Stanford from St. Asaph
In spring 1775 Benjamin Logan and John Floyd’s survey party camped at Buffalo Spring and planted St. Asaph; Logan’s stockade (Logan’s Fort) rose there by early 1777 and never fell—nicknamed “Standing Fort” after the May 1777 siege. Lincoln County courts moved to the fort in 1781; on May 16, 1786, the court permanently seated on Logan’s fifty-acre gift a mile east and named the town Stanford (often linked to “Standing Fort,” though Stamford, England, is also guessed). The courthouse site still holds some of Kentucky’s oldest continuous records, and Stanford remains Lincoln’s seat—second-oldest permanent settlement after Harrodsburg.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Stanford,_Kentucky

🪶 Mingo camps south of the Ohio
“Mingo” was the settlers’ label for Haudenosaunee (especially Seneca and Cayuga) families who had drifted into the Ohio Country and acted apart from the Onondaga council. With Shawnee, Cherokee, and Tutelo neighbors, Mingo hunters kept winter camps and small communities in the Bluegrass and middle Ohio Valley into the mid-1700s even after larger towns sat north of the river. After Fort Stanwix (1768) drew the Ohio as the line, those southern camps thinned; by the 1770s Mingo men more often appeared in raiding parties against the stations—Iroquoian hunters on Kentucky ground, not a generic “Indian” blur.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingo

🏺 Newt Kash Hollow (15Mf1)
In Menifee County’s Red River country, Newt Kash rockshelter preserved Early Woodland plant beds that reshaped how archaeologists talk about eastern agriculture. Webb and Funkhouser’s 1930s digs, then Volney Jones’s ethnobotany, recovered matting, grass bedding, squash, sunflower, goosefoot, and sumpweed—some of the earliest clear domesticates from a Kentucky shelter, occupied intensively around 3,000 years ago. Later readings treat the fiber, cradleboard, mortar, and medicinal plants as a possible women’s retreat and medicine place, not just a hunter’s overnight stop.
Source: https://doi.org/10.7183/0002-7316.76.4.628

🏺 Muir Site (15Js86)
West of Nicholasville on a Jessamine County ridge, Muir is an early Fort Ancient (Osborne phase) hamlet dated roughly A.D. 1000–1200. UK crews dug it in 1985 ahead of the Nicholasville Bypass and found only one to three small square-to-rectangular basin houses in use at a time—dispersed households with central hearths, limestone-tempered jars, and long-eared triangular points—rather than a later plaza village of fifteen or twenty houses. Deer, elk, and bear supplied most meat; corn, beans, and nuts filled the rest. Muir shows Bluegrass Fort Ancient life before the big nucleated towns.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Muir.aspx
