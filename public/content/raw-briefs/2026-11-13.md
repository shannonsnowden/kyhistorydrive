# Gen. Green Clay, Barbourville, Aristides, Cold Oak & the Catawba

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Friday, November 13, 2026*

🧭 **Gen. Green Clay, the surveyor who built Clermont**
Green Clay was born in Virginia in 1757 and came out to Kentucky as a surveyor around 1780. Virginia had issued overlapping land warrants, so a man who could run a clean line was in demand and was often paid in land. Clay ended up holding more than 40,000 acres, mostly in Madison County. He represented Madison in the Virginia House of Delegates (1788–89) and voted for the Constitution at Virginia's 1788 ratifying convention. He later sat in Kentucky's House and Senate. He also built the businesses that move a frontier county: ferries (Clay's Ferry on the Kentucky River), taverns, mills, distilleries and toll roads. In May 1813, as a major general of militia, he took about 1,200 Kentuckians down the Maumee in flatboats to reinforce Fort Meigs. White Hall, the house he began around 1799 near Richmond and later the home of his son Cassius M. Clay, is now a state historic site.

🏛️ **Barbourville, the Knox County seat on the Cumberland**
Dr. Thomas Walker built an 8-by-12-foot log cabin about five miles west of the future town in 1750, half a century before Barbourville existed. On Dec. 19, 1799, the legislature carved Knox County out of Lincoln County and named it for Henry Knox. Its county seat was laid out on the Cumberland River, on land given by James Barbour, and the town dates its start to 1799–1800. Because it sat on Wilderness Road traffic between Cumberland Gap and the Bluegrass, it is counted as the oldest town in Kentucky's mountains. By 1840 it had five stores, a tavern, three churches, a school and the courthouse. On Sept. 19, 1861, about 800 Confederates under Col. Joel A. Battle overran roughly 300 Union home guards at Camp Andy Johnson by the Cumberland River bridge. It was one of the first Civil War fights in the state.

🏇 **Horse series: Aristides, winner of the first Kentucky Derby**
This chapter picks up the bloodline from last week's Lexington at Woodburn: Aristides' dam, Sarong, was a daughter of Lexington. The chestnut colt with a star and two white socks was foaled in 1872 at H. Price McGrath's McGrathiana farm in Fayette County. McGrath named him for his friend Aristides Welch. Trainer Ansel Williamson had been born enslaved in Virginia around 1806, and Woodburn's R. A. Alexander bought him and set him free. Williamson and McGrath meant Aristides to be the pacesetter for his stablemate Chesapeake. On Monday, May 17, 1875, about 10,000 people came to the new Louisville Jockey Club track, later Churchill Downs. At the head of the stretch McGrath saw Chesapeake was out of it and waved 19-year-old jockey Oliver Lewis on. Aristides won by two lengths, ran the 1½ miles in an American-record 2:37¾ for a 3-year-old, and earned $2,850.

🏺 **Cold Oak Shelter, Lee County**
Cold Oak is a rock overhang in Lee County. People used it mainly at the end of the Late Archaic, about 1200–1000 BC, and they came back at different seasons through the year. Storage pits held hickory nuts, walnuts, acorns and chestnuts. They also held seeds of crops: sunflower, goosefoot, knotweed, marshelder, squash and possibly maygrass. The finds show people still hunting and gathering but leaning more on their own gardens by about 1200 BC. Preserved cordage, a wooden tool that may have been used for weaving, wood chips and a celt fragment suggest textile work and woodworking at the shelter. Kristen Gremillion's 1993 study of Cold Oak helped argue that the Eastern Agricultural Complex took shape in the Kentucky uplands, not just the river bottoms. This is a protected site. The pin is county-level. Occupation years are about 1200–1000 BC.

🗺️ **The Catawba and the northern war parties**
The Siouan-speaking Catawba lived on the Carolina Piedmont, not in Kentucky, but their wars were one reason so many war parties crossed Kentucky. For most of the 1700s they fought the Iroquois, Shawnee and Cherokee. After the French and the Iroquois made the "Great Peace" in 1701, the French steered Iroquois war parties south against the Carolinas and their Indian allies. Northern parties crossing the Ohio at the Scioto mouth picked up the Warriors' Path straight to Cumberland Gap and could continue on the Catawba Trail into the Carolinas. In the early 1750s, six Shawnee warriors on their way to strike the Catawba were jailed in South Carolina, and one of them, Itawachcomequa ("The Pride"), died there. One historian argues that death is what turned the Ohio Shawnee against the British colonies in 1754, opening a fight with the "Long Knives" that ran on and off for sixty years. The pin is the Warriors' Path in Kentucky, not a Catawba town.
