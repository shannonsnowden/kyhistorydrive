🤠 William Hoy and Hoy’s Station
William Hoy established Hoy’s Station in Madison County in 1781, with help from George Boone, according to a Boone-family genealogy; a Filson Historical Society study of pioneer forts and stations places it about six miles northwest of present-day Richmond. In 1782, Wyandot warriors captured two boys, Jones Hoy and Jack Calloway, near Boonesborough. On August 12, 1782, Capt. John Holder and 17 militiamen overtook the band on Great Salt Creek (the Licking River); Holder lost four men in the skirmish and, outnumbered, withdrew without the boys. Jones Hoy was held captive for seven years, Calloway not as long. The Kentucky Historical Society’s Upper Blue Licks marker (Nicholas County) records that skirmish but does not mention the station or say how the boys were tied to it, so the map pin uses the USGS historic-locale point for William Hoy’s Station—an approximate site.

🏘️ Shaker Village of Pleasant Hill
In 1805 three Shaker missionaries—John Meacham, Issachar Bates and Benjamin Seth Youngs—left New York and traveled mostly on foot to Kentucky, and in August found three Kentuckians willing to listen, who soon became the state’s first Shaker converts. Believers began moving to Elisha Thomas’s 140-acre Mercer County farm; 44 people signed the first family covenant in December 1806, and two years later they moved to a nearby hilltop they named Pleasant Hill. By 1823 the village had 491 Shakers on about 4,500 acres, and over 105 years they built more than 260 structures, including one of Kentucky’s earliest municipal water systems. By 1816 they were shipping brooms, cooperage, preserves and seeds on trading trips to New Orleans by river. Pleasant Hill closed as an active religious society by 1910 and the last Shaker died in 1923; a nonprofit formed in 1961 has since restored the village.

🎖️ Camp Nelson, Jessamine County
The U.S. Army established Camp Nelson in central Kentucky in April 1863 as a fortified supply depot, forward base and hospital, part of the Union effort to liberate East Tennessee. At its 1865 height it covered roughly 4,000 acres and more than 300 buildings, with earthen forts built primarily by enslaved African Americans impressed by the Army. From 1864 thousands of Black men, women and children reached the camp; eight regiments of United States Colored Troops were organized there, making it the largest USCT recruitment center in Kentucky and the third largest in the nation. In November 1864 soldiers expelled more than 400 women and children and destroyed their huts, and more than one hundred of the displaced refugees died of illness and exposure. The Army then built a Home for Colored Refugees, opened in January 1865, and on March 3, 1865 an Act of Congress emancipated the wives, children and mothers of USCT soldiers. Camp Nelson became a national monument in 2018; the pin marks the monument, not the whole camp.

🏛️ Andalex Village (15Hk22), Hopkins County
The Andalex site sits on a bluff overlooking the Pond River in Hopkins County, where Mississippian farmers occupied a mound center from AD 1175 to 1275. Cultural Resource Analysts investigated it in 1988 and 1989, before a coal mine was developed, and documented a platform mound, at least eleven houses around a central plaza, and a palisade enclosing the community. The mound ended up five feet tall and about 75 by 85 feet; it grew in three stages as the leader’s house on top was rebuilt and the mound capped with clay. Residents made shell-tempered Mississippi Plain jars, some with incised geometric designs on the necks (Pond River Incised), hunted mainly deer, grew corn, marshelder, maygrass and lambsquarters, and collected hickory nuts. The state archaeology site does not publish the location, so the pin is a county-level point for Hopkins County.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Andalex.aspx

🛣️ Louisville and Portland Canal
The Falls of the Ohio form the only barrier to navigation between Pittsburgh and New Orleans, and cargo was portaged around them except for a few weeks of high water each spring. Kentucky chartered the private Louisville and Portland Canal Company in 1825; construction started March 1, 1826, and the canal opened in 1830. As built, it was 1.9 miles long and 64 feet wide and ended in a three-flight lock with a total lift of 26 feet. Wikipedia describes it as the first major improvement completed on a major U.S. river and dates full completion to 1833. The route was later enlarged and rebuilt and is now the McAlpine Locks and Dam canal; the pin marks the lock site at the downstream end.
