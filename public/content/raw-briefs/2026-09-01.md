John Floyd
Virginia surveyor John Floyd led a 1774 Fincastle party into Kentucky, staked 2,000 acres on Beargrass Creek, and came back in 1779 to stockade Floyd's Station near today's St. Matthews. Virginia made him one of Louisville's first seven trustees, colonel of the Jefferson militia, and in 1783 one of Kentucky's first two judges. On April 8 that year he rode toward Bullitt's Lick in a scarlet coat bought in Paris. A shot from ambush dropped him. He died two days later, at 32. Floyd County, Floyds Fork, and the station springhouse still carry his name.

Georgetown and Royal Spring
In July 1774 Floyd claimed a thousand acres around a big Bluegrass spring. John McClelland of Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania, built a cabin above it in 1775 and threw up a stockade. Floyd later transferred the tract after finding the family already on the ground. An attack on December 29, 1776, killed McClelland, and the station emptied in 1777. Baptist preacher Elijah Craig brought a congregation from Orange County, Virginia, laid out Lebanon on the same spring, won a 1784 Virginia charter, and in 1790 renamed the place George Town for Washington. Scott County's seat still drinks from Royal Spring, the karst water that sited the town.

Treaties of Hard Labour and Lochaber
A month before Fort Stanwix, British superintendent John Stuart met Cherokee headmen at Hard Labour Creek, South Carolina. In mid-October 1768 they drew a line from Chiswell's lead mine to the Kanawha's mouth at today's Point Pleasant, putting both banks of the Kanawha in Virginia. Two years later at Lochaber (October 18, 1770) the Cherokee ceded still more. Surveyor John Donelson, walking the line in 1771 with Attakullakulla, then ran it not to the Kanawha but to the mouth of the Kentucky River (then often called the Louisa), a swing of roughly ten million acres. Shawnee hunters who actually used the Bluegrass were not signatories.

Indian Fort Mountain
On a knobby ridge south of Berea, dry-laid stone walls ring Indian Fort Mountain (15Ma25) in the Berea College Forest. Hopewell-era Middle Woodland people built them about 2,000 years ago as a hilltop enclosure, more a ceremonial marker than a stockade. Tests in 1980 treated the tumbled rock as linear mounds rather than a fighting wall. The site went on the National Register in 1983. Hikers still climb the Pinnacles over the same ground.

Leestown
In 1773 the McAfee company and Hancock Taylor surveyed a buffalo crossing on the Kentucky River about a mile north of today's Frankfort. Hancock and Willis Lee planted a village on the north bank in 1775, the first Anglo-American settlement north of the river. Virginia recognized it in 1776. Raids emptied it in 1777, then it came back as a tobacco-inspection warehouse (1783) and a shipping port for hemp, corn, and whiskey bound for New Orleans. It even contended for the capital. In 1827 the limestone for the Old State House was quarried from the riverbank here.
