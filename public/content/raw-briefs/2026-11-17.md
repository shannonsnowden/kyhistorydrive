# Peter Cartwright, Madisonville, I. W. Bernheim, Fort Jefferson & the Treaty of Lancaster

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Tuesday, November 17, 2026*

🤠 **Peter Cartwright, the backwoods preacher from Logan County**
Peter Cartwright was born in Amherst County, Virginia, on September 1, 1785. In the fall of 1793, after two years on a rented farm in Lincoln County, his family settled in Logan County, nine miles south of Russellville near the Tennessee line, in the corner of Kentucky then called Rogues' Harbor. He grew up racing horses and playing cards. In 1801, in his sixteenth year, he was converted at a sacramental meeting held by James McGready during the Great Revival, and in May 1802 he was given a permit to exhort. He was ordained a deacon in 1806 and an elder in 1808 and rode Methodist circuits in Kentucky and Tennessee for two decades. Opposed to slavery, he moved to free-state Illinois in 1824. In 1846 he ran for Congress and lost to Abraham Lincoln. His 1856 Autobiography is still one of the best firsthand pictures of Kentucky camp-meeting life.

🏙️ **Madisonville, a courthouse town on McGary and Silkwood land**
The legislature carved Hopkins County out of Henderson County on December 9, 1806, and named it for Gen. Samuel Hopkins. The next year, 1807, Madisonville was laid out as the county seat and named for James Madison, then Secretary of State. Daniel McGary and Solomon Silkwood, who between them held about 1,000 acres along what became Main Street, gave the 40-acre town site. A plain courthouse went up that same year, and the town was incorporated in 1810. Much of the early settlement came from Revolutionary War veterans holding Virginia land grants southwest of the Green River. Tobacco carried the town first. Then came the first local coal mine in 1869 and the railroad in 1870, which turned the county seat into a coal and rail center.

🥃 **I. W. Bernheim, from peddler to I. W. Harper**
Bourbon Series. Isaac Wolfe Bernheim landed in New York from Baden in 1867 with about four dollars. He peddled in Pennsylvania until his horse died, then went to Paducah as a clerk and bookkeeper for a liquor wholesaler. On January 1, 1872, he and his brother Bernard opened Bernheim Brothers there. Their premium label was I. W. Harper. They moved to Louisville's Main Street, between First and Second, in 1888. After an 1896 fire at a Pleasure Ridge Park distillery, they built their own plant near the city limits on the Illinois Central Railroad, which made its first mash in April 1897. In 1928 Bernheim bought cut-over Bullitt County land at $1 an acre, which became Bernheim Forest.

🏰 **Fort Jefferson: Clark's fort on Chickasaw ground**
George Rogers Clark reached the mouth of the Ohio on April 19, 1780, and his Illinois Battalion built Fort Jefferson in what is now Ballard County, with a civilian town, Clarksville, beside it. Gov. Thomas Jefferson approved the post on condition that the land be bought from the Chickasaw, but Clark never bought it or asked their consent. Chickasaw warriors attacked the village on July 17, 1780, and from August 27 to 30 about 150 Chickasaw and two British provincials besieged the post, destroying the corn and the last livestock. Families left that fall, Virginia could not supply the fort, and the garrison withdrew to Louisville on June 8, 1781. In 1782 four Chickasaw chiefs wrote that the Americans had built a fort in their hunting grounds without permission and had "suffered most from us." The map shows it at Ballard County level (protected site), 1780–1781.

🪶 **The Treaty of Lancaster, 1744: how Virginia claimed Kentucky**
The Iroquois Confederacy based its claim to the Ohio Valley on its Beaver Wars conquests. At Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in June and July 1744, the Six Nations gave Virginia a deed recognizing the King's right to all the lands of the colony, for goods and £200 in gold. The two sides read that deal very differently. Virginia said it covered everything inside its 1609 charter, west to the Mississippi, Kentucky included. The Iroquois said they had sold only the land between the Blue Ridge and the Alleghenies. The 1752 Treaty of Logstown and the 1768 Treaty of Fort Stanwix papered over the gap, but the Shawnee and Cherokee kept their own claims.
