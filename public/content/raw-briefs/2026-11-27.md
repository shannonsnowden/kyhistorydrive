# Isaac Hite, Smithland, Grey Eagle and Wagner, the Crigler Mounds & the New Madrid Earthquakes

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Friday, November 27, 2026*

🤠 **Isaac Hite of Cave Spring**
Isaac Hite was born in 1753 in Hampshire County, Virginia, and came down the Ohio in 1773 to join Capt. Thomas Bullitt's surveying party, the first to survey the land at the Falls where Louisville now stands. He came back in 1774, helped establish Harrodsburg, and lived there until about 1781. In 1775 the Boiling Spring settlement sent him to the Transylvania convention at Boonesborough. He fought in the defense of Boonesborough and was wounded on April 24, 1777, and he served under George Rogers Clark in the campaigns of 1780 and 1782. In 1781 he was named one of the first justices of a Kentucky county court, and he worked as a deputy surveyor in five counties. On Goose Creek east of the Falls he built Cave Spring, with a mill and a tannery; John Filson's 1784 map marked it as Hite's Mill. He signed his will on February 8, 1794, and died that year.

🚢 **Smithland: Zachariah Cox's town at the mouth of the Cumberland**
Georgia land speculator Zachariah Cox had the country at the mouth of the Cumberland explored in 1796 by Capt. John Smith, announced a commercial settlement there at Knoxville in April 1797, and told Gov. James Garrard of his plans at Frankfort in January 1798. Gen. James Wilkinson at Fort Massac, who had trading plans of his own with the Spanish, persuaded federal officials that Cox was building a boat for piracy and war against the Indians. Cox was arrested at Natchez on August 18, 1798, escaped, was jailed again in Nashville, and was cleared, but he lost his company. The settlement took Smith's name. Smithland was laid out on a plat in 1805, recorded in 1837, and became the Livingston County seat after Crittenden County was formed in 1842.

🐎 **Horse Series: Grey Eagle and Wagner at the Oakland Course**
On September 30, 1839, Kentucky's Grey Eagle, a four-year-old gray foaled near Lexington in 1835, met Wagner, a Virginia-bred chestnut, at Louisville's Oakland Course for a purse reported at $14,000, best two of three four-mile heats. About 10,000 people watched Wagner, ridden by the enslaved jockey Cato, win both heats in 7:48 and 7:44. Five days later they met again for a $1,500 Jockey Club purse. Grey Eagle won the first heat, Wagner the second by a neck in 7:43, and in the third heat Grey Eagle broke down. He never raced again. Edward Troye painted both horses, and the 1843 engraving of Wagner shows Cato at his head.

🏺 **Crigler Mounds: Adena Burial Mounds in Boone County**
In Boone County, Adena people raised two burial mounds, one large and one small, known as sites 15BE20 and 15BE27. Beneath the large mound lay a circle of paired posts 56 feet across, with a raised clay platform across from an entrance. The excavators, working with Works Progress Administration labor in the early 1940s, read it as a meeting place set apart from where people lived; later archaeologists have seen such circles as mortuary camps or open-air precincts. The Adena tradition flourished in the central Ohio Valley from about 600 BCE to about 200 CE. The mounds are protected archaeological sites and are shown only at the county level.
Source: [Adena culture on Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Adena_culture)

🌎 **The New Madrid earthquakes in Kentucky, 1811–12**
At 2:15 a.m. on December 16, 1811, the first of the New Madrid earthquakes shook Louisville, and a stronger shock followed after 7 a.m. Gable ends, parapets and chimneys fell. Louisville resident Jared Brooks hung pendulums from 1 to 6 inches long and set springs to catch the motion, ranked each shock on a six-step scale, and counted 1,874 shocks felt at Louisville from December 16, 1811, to March 15, 1812. In February 1812 the ground rose under the Mississippi at the Kentucky Bend and briefly made waterfalls. His record was published in 1819 in Sketches of Louisville and Its Environs.
