# Surveyors, Crossroads, and a Miner Under the Rock

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Thursday, November 5, 2026*

🧭 **Capt. Thomas Bullitt's 1773 survey party**
In the summer of 1773, Virginia officer Thomas Bullitt (c. 1730–1778) led one of the first surveying parties into the Kentucky country. About thirty-four men set out; Isaac Hite and six others joined them on the Ohio, and the McAfee brothers split off at Big Bone Lick to survey on their own. Bullitt's party reached the Falls of the Ohio in July 1773 and ran surveys under claims from the Proclamation of 1763, even though Governor Dunmore wrote that he "knew nothing" of the work and wanted him back. Bullitt is credited with locating the salt spring on Salt River later called Bullitt's Lick. Old family accounts say he also laid off Louisville in 1773, but an 1882 Falls-cities history found no reliable plat. Louisville's half-acre lots came with the settlers of 1778 and a 1779 survey. Bullitt County is named for his nephew, Alexander Scott Bullitt. ([Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Bullitt))

🏘️ **Mount Washington grows from "The Crossroads"**
Northeast Bullitt County's main town started as a junction, not a fort. Settlers called it "The Crossroads" because the Bardstown–Louisville road crossed the Shepherdsville–Shelbyville road there, near McCullough's Run in the Salt River country. It was renamed Mount Vernon, then became Mount Washington when it incorporated in 1833, which avoided a post-office name conflict. By 1847 about 700 people lived there, with three churches, two schools, six stores, five doctors, and two taverns serving traffic between Louisville and Bardstown. Confederate cavalry briefly held the town in the fall of 1862 before Union troops marching toward Perryville drove them out. The road it sat on still runs from Shelby County. ([Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Mount_Washington,_Kentucky))

🪨 **"Lost John," the miner under the boulder in Mammoth Cave**
In 1935, guides deep in Mammoth Cave found the body of an adult man pinned under a huge boulder. He was a pre-Columbian miner who had been digging out cave minerals and disturbed the rubble holding the rock up, and it shifted and settled onto him. The dry cave air naturally mummified him, and the artifacts found with him point to an age of more than 2,000 years. "Lost John," along with remains from other caves nearby, shows how far Woodland-era Kentuckians went underground and what it could cost them. He was displayed to visitors into the 1970s and then reburied in a secret spot inside the cave. ([Grokipedia — Mammoth Cave National Park](https://grokipedia.com/page/Mammoth_Cave_National_Park))

🪶 **Tecumseh falls at the Thames, Oct. 5, 1813**
Kentucky's frontier wars with the Shawnee-led confederacy ended in Ontario. After the American win on Lake Erie, Governor Isaac Shelby, then 63 and a veteran of Kings Mountain, personally led more than 3,500 Kentucky volunteers, mostly mounted riflemen, north to join William Henry Harrison. On October 5, 1813, near Moraviantown on the Thames River, Col. Richard Mentor Johnson's Kentucky horsemen charged through swampy ground and broke Procter's British line. Tecumseh was killed fighting in the woods next to it. His multi-tribal alliance collapsed after his death, which effectively ended organized Native resistance in the Old Northwest and the last Native threat to Kentucky's northern border. ([Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Battle_of_the_Thames))

🛤️ **Zane's Trace ends at Maysville**
In May 1796, Congress backed Col. Ebenezer Zane's plan to cut a road through the Northwest Territory "to speed up travel times to Kentucky." Built in 1796–97 with his brother Jonathan, son-in-law John McIntire, and a Native guide named Tomepomehala, the road followed old Indian paths such as the Mingo and Moxahala trails. It ran more than 230 miles from Wheeling to the Ohio River across from Maysville (old Limestone). For settlers who didn't want to float the river, it made Maysville the overland door into the Bluegrass. That traffic helps explain why the road south from Maysville to Lexington mattered so much later. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zane%27s_Trace))
