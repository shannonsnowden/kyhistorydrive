Capt. James Estill / Estill's Defeat
On March 22, 1782, Capt. James Estill and about 25 Lincoln County militiamen overtook a Wyandot raiding party at Little Mountain Creek, near today's Mount Sterling. After nearly two hours of fighting across the creek, Lt. William Miller's left flank broke, the Wyandots closed, and Estill was killed in hand-to-hand combat along with six of his men. The fight—also called the Battle of Little Mountain—was one of the bloodiest single engagements of Kentucky's "Year of Blood," and Estill County was later named for him.

Mount Sterling / Little Mountain Town
Explorers out of Boonesborough called a tree-covered Adena burial mound "the Little Mountain." Scotsman Hugh Forbes began selling lots beside it around 1790; in 1792 the Kentucky Assembly chartered the town on 640 acres from Forbes, Enoch Smith, John Judy, and Samuel Spurgin. Forbes meant Mount Stirling (for Stirling, Scotland, plus the mound); the misspelled "Sterling" stuck. The mound—once about 25 feet high at Queen and Locust—was leveled in 1845, and Mount Sterling became Montgomery County's seat.

Treaty of Camp Charlotte (1774)
After the Battle of Point Pleasant on October 10, 1774, Shawnee leaders under Cornstalk met Virginia's Lord Dunmore at Camp Charlotte on the Scioto. In the treaty that closed Dunmore's War, the Shawnee accepted the Ohio River as the boundary and gave up hunting rights south of it—Kentucky included. That temporary quiet opened the door for the Transylvania purchase and the 1775 rush of stations, even though raids resumed once the Revolution pulled British and tribal alliances back into the Ohio Valley.

Carlston Annis Shell Mound
On the Green River floodplain in Butler County, Carlston Annis (15Bt5) is a Late Archaic shell midden about 300 by 350 feet and more than six feet thick, piled up beside a mussel shoal between roughly 3000 and 1000 BC. WPA crews under William S. Webb dug nearly half an acre in 1940–41; later work by the Shell Mound Archaeological Project refined the picture. The site yielded hundreds of burials, dog burials, hearths, pestle caches, and deer-bone pins—evidence of repeated seasonal camps, not a one-night stop.

Jacob Spears & early "Bourbon" whiskey
Pennsylvania-born Revolutionary veteran Jacob Spears settled near Paris in Bourbon County and was distilling by the 1790s. Thomas Metcalfe built his stone house ("Stone Castle") around 1790; the farm still holds a springhouse and whiskey storehouse—Kentucky's most complete early distiller's complex. Spears floated barrels down the Licking to the Ohio and on to New Orleans, and he is often credited among the first to market the county's whiskey under the name that stuck: bourbon.
