# Kentucky History Morning Update — 2026-09-21

This morning's Kentucky history brief:

Cloth, County Seats & Wabash Raids

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Monday, September 21, 2026*

🤠 Ann McGinty and Kentucky's first spinning wheel
Anne Kennedy (later Poage, Lindsay, and McGinty) crossed into Kentucky in September 1775 with a spinning wheel—the first brought over Cumberland Gap—and soon helped her husband William Poage build looms at Harrodstown. Their linsey-woolsey cloth became a frontier staple; she later ran an ordinary (tavern-inn) and is buried in the Fort Harrod Pioneer Cemetery (d. 1815).
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/983

🏙 Lawrenceburg: from Kaufman's Station to Anderson County seat
German immigrant Jacob Kaufman settled the site about 1780 as Kaufman's (Coffman's) Station. A post office opened in 1817 for tavern-keeper William Lawrence; Franklin County incorporated the town as Lawrence in 1820. When Anderson County was cut from Franklin, Mercer, and Washington in 1827, the place was renamed Lawrenceburg and made the county seat.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Lawrenceburg,_Kentucky

🏹 Wea towns at Ouiatenon and Scott's 1791 Kentucky raid
The Wea (Waayaahtanwa), Miami–Illinois speakers, made Ouiatenon on the Wabash their principal town and a French fur-trade hub after Beaver Wars displacements. In June 1791, Gen. Charles Scott led about 800 Kentucky mounted volunteers from Limestone (Maysville) and burned Wea and Kickapoo villages plus hundreds of acres of corn—a scorched-earth strike in the Northwest Indian War that scattered the hub without a pitched battle.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouiatenon

🏛 Adams Site Mississippian town near Hickman
Adams (15Fu4) on Bayou de Chien in Fulton County is a roughly 7-hectare Mississippian center with platform mounds and plazas built over an earlier Late Woodland village. Occupied about 1100–1500 CE (Medley and Jackson phases), it left deep village middens up to 1.5 meters thick and was listed on the National Register in 1984.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams_site

🥃 Henry McKenna's Fairfield sour mash
Irish immigrant Henry McKenna of County Derry settled at Fairfield in Nelson County, began distilling in a mill shed by 1855 (under a barrel a day), and built a brick distillery by 1883. He sold only "H. McKenna Old Line" sour mash—never green whisky under three years—and stamped barrels with an Irish harp. The brand survived Prohibition as medicinal whisky; Heaven Hill later revived it.
