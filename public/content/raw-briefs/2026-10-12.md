🤠 Capt. Robert Johnson and Big Crossing Station
Robert Johnson (1745–1815) came from Orange County, Virginia, bought part of Col. John Floyd’s 1774 North Elkhorn survey, and first reached Kentucky in 1779. After a season on Beargrass and time at Bryan’s Station—where tradition holds his wife Jemima Suggett Johnson led the women to the spring before the August 1782 siege—he founded Big Crossing Station in 1783 at the buffalo ford on North Elkhorn, about two miles west of today’s Georgetown.

It was Scott County’s first permanent settlement, picketed in, with a horse mill and then a water mill by 1784. Johnson sat in the Virginia Assembly for Fayette, surveyed widely, helped defend the frontier through the early 1790s, served in both Kentucky constitutional conventions (1792 and 1799), and with Jemima donated land for Great Crossing Baptist Church.

Three of their sons sat in Congress in 1825; Richard M. Johnson became vice president in 1837. Robert and Jemima are buried in the church cemetery at Great Crossing. The map pin is Great Crossing on North Elkhorn.

🏙️ Campbellsville: from Buckhorn Creek lots to a county seat
Andrew Campbell of Augusta County, Virginia, was selling lots on Buckhorn Creek, a Green River tributary, by 1814. He built a gristmill and a tavern, and with brothers Adam, Michael, James, and David pushed a town that the Kentucky General Assembly established on Jan. 3, 1817, still then in Green County.

A post office opened the day before with Pleasant Kirtley as postmaster; by 1820 the plat showed about 85 lots around a public square. When Taylor County was cut from Green in 1848—Kentucky’s 100th county, named for Gen. Zachary Taylor—Campbellsville became the seat, and the square was sold to the county for a dollar for the first courthouse.

The town sits near the state’s claimed geographic center and remains the Taylor County hub. The map pin marks the town.

🪶 Blackfish, Chillicothe war chief
Blackfish (c. 1729–1779), Cot-ta-wa-ma-go or Mkah-day-way-may-qua in Shawnee, was war chief of the Chillicothe division. After Cornstalk’s murder at Fort Randolph in November 1777, he led British-allied Shawnee pressure on the Kentucky stations—defending Shawnee hunting grounds south of the Ohio that settlers were claiming under Virginia patents the Shawnee had never consented to give up.

In February 1778 his party took Daniel Boone and the Blue Licks saltmakers; Boone was adopted as Sheltowee (“Big Turtle”) and held about four months before escaping to warn Boonesborough. That September Blackfish brought several hundred Shawnee and allied warriors, with British supplies, against the fort in an eleven-day siege that failed.

In May 1779 Col. John Bowman’s Kentucky militia struck Chillicothe (near present Xenia, Ohio); Blackfish helped drive them off but took a leg wound that turned septic and killed him later that year—removing one of the Shawnee’s toughest field leaders just as the Ohio Valley war intensified. The map pin is the Boonesborough siege site.

🏺 Tinsley Hill: Mississippian town under Lake Barkley
On the Cumberland in Lyon County, Tinsley Hill (15Ly18) was a late Mississippian town with two mounds, a village, and a stone-box cemetery. Douglas W. Schwartz excavated the cemetery in 1958–60 for the University of Kentucky; R. Berle Clay dug the village in 1960 and 1962 under the Inter-Agency Archaeological Salvage Program before Lake Barkley rose.

The cemetery alone yielded remains of well over a hundred people and hundreds of associated objects. Ceramics from the later occupation define the Tinsley Hill Phase, dated roughly AD 1300–1450 in the lower Tennessee–Cumberland sequence—farmers and town-builders on western Kentucky ground centuries before the first English surveys.

The site now lies under Lake Barkley; the map pin is a generalized public point for the inundated Lyon County stretch of the Cumberland, not an exact site coordinate.

🏛️ Burlington: from Craig’s Camp to Boone’s seat
Boone County’s first court met June 17, 1799, at William Cave’s house in North Bend Bottoms and fixed the seat on 74 acres at the headwaters of Allens Fork—Craig’s Camp, named for John Hawkins Craig. On Oct. 20, 1800, Craig and Robert Johnson established a town there called Wilmington; court sat in a log courthouse by January 1801, and Moses Scott platted the grid around a central Public Square that September.

The U.S. Post Office asked for a rename in 1816 to avoid another Wilmington, and Burlington stuck. The 1889 Renaissance Revival courthouse still serves; the historic district landed on the National Register in 1979.

Burlington later lost its city charter and remains one of Kentucky’s two unincorporated county seats, still the place for courts, clerk, and jail in Boone County. The map pin marks the town.
