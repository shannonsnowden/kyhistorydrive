🤠 Hugh McGary, the hothead major
Irish-born Hugh McGary (1744–1806) came through the Gap with early Kentucky families, settled near Harrodsburg, and built McGary’s Station at Shawnee Springs in Mercer County around 1779. His temper made him useful in a fight and disastrous in counsel: at Blue Licks in August 1782 he dared the militia across the Licking (“Them that ain’t damned cowards, follow me”), helping trigger the ambush that killed dozens, including Israel Boone. Four years later, on Logan’s Mad River raid, he tomahawked the surrendering Shawnee chief Moluntha against orders and drew a court-martial—still Kentucky’s clearest portrait of a pioneer whose courage and rage ran on the same fuse.

🏙️ Lancaster at Wallace’s Crossroads
When Garrard County was carved in December 1796 (Kentucky’s 25th county, named for Gov. James Garrard), commissioners needed a seat where water and roads already met. Capt. William Buford donated about 57 acres at Wallace’s Crossroads—where the Harrodsburg–Boonesborough road crossed the Hickman–Crab Orchard route—and Joseph Bledsoe Jr. platted a grid around a public square in 1797–98. First court sat June 6, 1797; a brick courthouse rose by 1799. The name “Lancaster” came from Pennsylvania (via settler Henry Paulding or the plan itself). The town stuck as Garrard’s only incorporated seat, still anchored on that pioneer crossroads.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster,_Kentucky

🪶 Erie, the Cat Nation
South of Lake Erie, the Eriehronon—“People of the Long Tail,” called La Nation du Chat by the French—farmed and traded until Haudenosaunee armies smashed their confederacy in 1654–56. Survivors were adopted, fled south as Rickahocan/Westo, or melted into Ohio Valley bands later labeled Mingo. That Iroquois victory widened claims over beaver country deep into the Ohio Valley; a century later English long hunters still met a Kentucky treated as contested hunting ground, not permanent towns—partly because nations like the Erie had already been swept aside.
Source: https://case.edu/ech/articles/e/erie-indians

🏺 Peter Village enclosure (15Fa166)
Just north of Lexington along the Mount Horeb complex, Peter Village is a huge, non-geometric ditch-and-embankment earthwork Rafinesque paced as a roughly twenty-sided polygon nearly 3,800 feet around. Early use (~300–200 BCE) looks pre-Adena: a stockade, then ditch and bank, tied to harvesting and working local galena and barite rather than a simple village. Later Adena people reused the enclosure briefly; recent LiDAR and radiocarbon work suggest it may be among the Middle Ohio Valley’s earliest ditch-and-embankment monuments, with a southern entrance and a second outer embankment still reading in the ground.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Horeb_Earthworks_Complex

🤠 Capt. Abraham Lincoln on Long Run
The president’s grandfather—Capt. Abraham Lincoln (1744–1786), Augusta/Rockingham militia officer and tanner—bought rich Jefferson County land near Hughes’ Station on Floyd’s Fork (Long Run) and moved the family from Virginia about 1781–82. In May 1786, while planting corn with sons Mordecai, Josiah, and young Thomas, he was shot from the timber. Mordecai ran for a rifle and killed the warrior reaching for Thomas; Josiah ran to the station for help. Tradition places the grave at today’s Long Run Baptist Church cemetery near Eastwood—the story the future president said was “more strongly than all others imprinted” on his memory.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln_(captain)
