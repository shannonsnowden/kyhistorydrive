John Filson / Kentucke 1784
Pennsylvania schoolteacher and surveyor John Filson reached Lexington around 1783 with land warrants for more than 12,000 acres. In 1784 he published The Discovery, Settlement and Present State of Kentucke—the first written history of the country—with a companion map engraved in Philadelphia. The book's appendix carried the earliest printed Boone narrative, drawn from Filson's interviews, and the map steered migrants through the Gap. In 1788 Filson helped plat Losantiville (later Cincinnati); that October he vanished while surveying near the Great Miami and was never found.

Winchester / Clark County seat
Clark County was carved from Fayette and Bourbon on December 6, 1792, and named for George Rogers Clark. Justices first met at John Strode's station, then voted to put the public buildings on John Baker's land by his spring. On December 17, 1793, the Assembly established a town there named Winchester, after Baker's hometown in Frederick County, Virginia. A two-room log courthouse went up in 1794; Winchester remains Clark's seat and still sits on the old path toward the mountains.

Mosopelea (Ofo) & the Ohio Valley
Before the Shawnee dominated the Ohio hunting grounds, Siouan-speaking Mosopelea (later called Ofo) held eight villages north of the Ohio between the Muskingum and Scioto. Franquelin's 1684 map marks those towns "destroyed." La Salle recorded Seneca-led Iroquois strikes in the early 1670s during the Beaver Wars; by 1673 Marquette and Joliet found the Mosopelea already fled down the Mississippi. Remnants lived among the Quapaw, Tunica, and Biloxi. Miami-Illinois speakers once called the Ohio Mosopeleacipi—"river of the Mosopelea"—a reminder that Kentucky's middle ground emptied more than once before Anglo stations rose.

Hardin Village (15Gp22)
On the Ohio floodplain in Greenup County near South Shore, Hardin Village was a late Fort Ancient town occupied from the late 1500s into the early 1600s and covering more than ten acres. University of Kentucky digs in 1939 and again in the 2010s mapped large bark-walled houses (about 50–70 by 24–29 feet), storage and trash pits, and family cemeteries with feasting hearths. Triangular endscrapers point to intensive hide work for long-distance exchange—marine shell and brass among the imports—just as the protohistoric Ohio Valley was tipping toward abandonment.

Lexington & Ohio Railroad
On January 27, 1830, Kentucky chartered the Lexington & Ohio—the commonwealth's first railroad, and among the first west of the Alleghenies—to tie Lexington to the Ohio River. Track used English strap iron spiked to quarried limestone sills; horses pulled early cars, then steam. The "Daniel Boone" engine made Lexington to Frankfort in about two and a half hours in 1835. Strap iron and stone crumbled under locomotives and were later replaced with wooden ties and T-rail, but the line cracked open Bluegrass freight toward the river long before the Civil War.
