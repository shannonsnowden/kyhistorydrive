# Kentucky History Morning Update — 2026-10-01

This morning's Kentucky history brief:

Long Hunters, Town Springs, and Saltpeter

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Thursday, October 1, 2026*

🤠 James Knox
Before Boone’s fame crowded the memory, Scots-Irish long hunter James Knox led parties through Cumberland Gap into Kentucky’s Green River country. Around 1770 his men built a “skin house” pelt camp on what is still called Skinhouse Branch in Green County—Camp Knox on the markers. Tradition holds that Cherokee hunter Captain Dick guided him and that Knox named Dix River for that guide. Knox later raised a company for Virginia’s 8th Regiment, fought with Morgan’s Rifles at Saratoga, and sat in the Kentucky Senate from Lincoln County (1795–1800).
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_hunter

🏙️ Somerset
Pulaski County needed a seat after its 1799 creation from Lincoln and Green. In 1801 the court laid out Somerset on forty acres donated by William Dodson, beside a dependable spring whose path became the town’s busiest early street. Migrants from Somerset County, New Jersey, gave the place its name. Kentucky Historical Society Marker 1607 still marks the Old Town Spring—where locals joked that whoever drank would gain wisdom and always return.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset,_Kentucky

🪶 Moneton
The Moneton (“Big Water” people) lived along the Kanawha and New Rivers in the middle Ohio Valley—on Kentucky’s eastern approaches—when English explorers first wrote them down. Thomas Batts noted them in 1671; Gabriel Arthur, sent by Abraham Wood from Fort Henry, described their “great town” in 1674. Likely tied to Fort Ancient networks and sometimes linked to Siouan neighbors, they vanish from the record soon after, absorbed under disease, Iroquois pressure, and the slave trade—many scholars argue into Shawnee communities—before Kentucky stations rose west of their homeland.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moneton

🏺 Croley-Evans Site
On the Upper Cumberland in Knox County, Croley-Evans (15KX24) was a Mississippian town-and-mound center at the northern edge of that world. UK archaeologist Richard Jefferies’s work shows the heaviest occupation from about A.D. 1200 to 1450: platform-mound stages, village areas, shell-tempered pottery, and a mixed farming-and-foraging economy with fewer exotic trade goods than richer southern centers. Listed on the National Register in 1985 (address restricted), it still marks how Mississippian life looked on Kentucky’s southeast frontier.
Source: https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/mississippian-adaptation-on-the-northern-periphery-settlement-sub/

⛏️ Mammoth Cave saltpeter
Long before tourism, Mammoth Cave’s nitrate-rich dirt fed gunpowder. In the War of 1812, when British blockade cut imported saltpeter, operators worked the Rotunda and nearby passages at industrial scale—leaching cave soil in wooden vats, pumping liquor to the surface, and converting it to potassium nitrate. Enslaved laborers did most of the hauling and filtering; remnants of vats, pipes, and pump works still stand just inside the Historic Entrance. After 1815 cheap imports killed the market, and the same passages became Kentucky’s first great show cave.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammoth_Cave_National_Park
