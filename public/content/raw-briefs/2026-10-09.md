🤠 John Bradford and the Kentucke Gazette
John Bradford (1749–1830), born in Prince William County, Virginia, first came to Kentucky with a surveying party in 1775 and moved his family out in 1784 or 1785. He had little experience as a printer or editor, but with Fielding Bradford he started Kentucky’s first newspaper in Lexington, the Kentucke Gazette. The first issue was printed on Aug. 11, 1787, and the paper switched to the modern spelling of the state’s name in 1789. It had no rival within 500 miles until 1795. After statehood, Bradford printed the first book published in Kentucky, a compilation of the acts of the legislature’s first session in 1792. He served as a Lexington town trustee, in the legislature, and as Fayette County sheriff, and he helped found Transylvania University, chairing its board of trustees from 1793 until his death in 1830. Beginning in August 1828 he wrote a series of 66 newspaper articles on Kentucky’s early years: the Indian wars, the fight for free use of the Mississippi and the port of New Orleans, and the ten conventions that led to statehood. Those “Notes” are still a valued source on the era. On his farm north of Lexington he built a Flemish-bond brick house called Fairfield, about 1785–1800. The map pin marks the Kentucky Historical Society’s John Bradford marker (#1864) at 1916 Iron Works Pike.

🏙️ Carlisle: a county seat in Kincart’s peach orchard
Nicholas County was formed in 1799 from parts of Mason and Bourbon counties and named for Col. George Nicholas, a Revolutionary War veteran who became a popular lawyer in early Kentucky. Its county court first met in 1800 at the home of Martin Baker Jr. at Lower Blue Licks, then in a new courthouse at Blue Licks. In 1805 the seat moved to a new town, Ellisville, laid out on James Ellis’s farm along the Lexington–Maysville road, where the county’s courthouse stood until 1816. That year the seat moved again. John Kincart, born in 1791 in what became Nicholas County, had inherited his father Samuel’s 200-acre farm in 1813, and in 1816 he gave 50 acres, including the family’s peach orchard, for a new town named Carlisle. Streets and lots were laid off around the center of the tract, and Kincart took town lots as payment for his land. The present Nicholas County Courthouse is the third on that site, and a monument to Samuel Kincart stands on its north side. Daniel Boone had lived in the county from 1795 to 1799, and his restored cabin in Carlisle is kept by the Carlisle Tourism Commission. The map pin marks the courthouse square.

🐎 Horse series: the Kentucky Association and Lexington’s race course
On July 23, 1826, Bluegrass horsemen formed the Kentucky Association to promote the breeding and racing of Thoroughbreds. Its founders included Henry Clay, Jesse Bledsoe, Dr. Elisha Warfield, and Thomas F. Marshall, and the Keeneland Library’s account says about fifty members met at Mrs. Keen’s Inn with the aim “to improve the breed of horses by encouraging the sports of the turf.” Between 1828 and 1834 the Association bought 65 acres at what is now the east end of Fifth Street at Race Street in Lexington and built the Lexington Race Course, a one-mile dirt track with a grandstand and stables. The Phoenix Hotel Handicap was first run there in 1831; as the Phoenix Stakes it is now the oldest stakes race in the United States. Lexington’s racing had long depended on enslaved grooms, trainers, and riders, and after emancipation Black horsemen stayed central at the track: future Hall of Fame jockey Isaac Murphy won his first race there on Sept. 16, 1876, and Jimmy Winkfield got his start there in 1898. Money troubles forced a sale in 1890. The Association closed the track in the spring of 1933, the same spring the Keeneland Association incorporated to build a new course outside town, and the old grounds were torn down in 1935. Keeneland copied the old track’s gates. The site is now part of Lexington’s East End, around William Wells Brown Elementary School, where street names recall the track. The map pin marks the Fifth and Race streets area.

🪶 Treaty of Logstown, 1752
Logstown (French Chiningué) stood on the Ohio River near present Ambridge and Baden in Beaver County, Pennsylvania. Shawnee people settled it around 1725 to 1730, and Lenape, Cayuga, Seneca, and more Shawnee families joined them after a smallpox epidemic in 1733 and a drought in 1741, making it a multi-ethnic town. In 1747 the Six Nations sent two headmen to oversee their allies there, the Seneca Tanacharison, called the Half King, and the Oneida Scarouady. In 1749 the Crown had granted the Ohio Company 500,000 acres between the Monongahela and Kanawha rivers, on condition that it settle 100 families within seven years. From June 1 to 13, 1752, Virginia commissioners Joshua Fry, James Patton, and Lunsford Lomax, with Christopher Gist, William Trent, and William Beverley for the Ohio Company, met representatives of the Six Nations and of the Lenape and Shawnee at Logstown. Virginia wanted the Six Nations to confirm the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster, which it read as a cession of land along the southeast side of the Ohio, and the company wanted leave to build a fort at the Forks. Tanacharison at first said the Lancaster sale reached no farther west than the Alleghenies, but he gave way, agreed to a fort at the mouth of the Monongahela, and promised that settlements southeast of the Ohio “shall be unmolested by us.” His word did not bind the Onondaga Council, and the Ohio peoples never agreed to English settlement; Scarouady warned, “we intend to keep our country clear of settlements.” France answered with a chain of new forts. For Kentucky, Logstown was an early paper claim to land south of the Ohio; Gist had already crossed into Kentucky on his Ohio Company survey in 1751. Scarouady burned the town in 1754, the French began rebuilding it in 1755, and by late 1758 its people had left for new towns on the Scioto. Logstown is in Pennsylvania, so this story has no map pin.

🪨 Mantle Rock and the Trail of Tears, 1838–39
West of the crossroads of Joy in Livingston County, off KY 133, a sandstone arch about 30 feet high and 188 feet long stands in the woods of the McGilligan Creek watershed, one of the longest natural arches in Kentucky. Nearby runs a stretch of the old Salem–Golconda Road, and in the winter of 1838–39 that road carried the Cherokee Nation’s forced removal on the northern route of the Trail of Tears. Eleven detachments, more than 10,000 Cherokee in all, came this way toward Berry’s Ferry on the Ohio and the crossing to Golconda, Illinois, not yet halfway to Indian Territory. Ice closed the river that winter. The Peter Hildebrand detachment, about 1,766 people, spent roughly two bitterly cold weeks in camps strung for miles along the road, waiting for the river to thaw enough for the ferry. A state historical marker says the Cherokee sheltered beneath the arch and that many died there; the National Park Service says there were far too many people to camp under it, that historical descriptions point to a long camp along the road, and that there is no evidence of burials at Mantle Rock. Across the river, many were trapped again between the frozen Ohio and Mississippi, and the detachments reached Oklahoma hundreds fewer than they started. The Nature Conservancy owns the 367-acre preserve. It was listed on the National Register in 2004 as the Mantle Rock Archeological District and certified that year as a site on the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail, and a 2.75-mile loop trail follows about a mile of the old road. The map pin marks the Kentucky Historical Society’s Mantle Rock marker (#1675) on KY 133.
