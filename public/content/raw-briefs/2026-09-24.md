# Kentucky History Morning Update — 2026-09-24

Jenny Wiley, Yellow Banks & the Cat Nation

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Thursday, September 24, 2026*

🤠 Jenny Wiley’s captivity and escape
On October 1, 1789, at Walker’s Creek in present-day Bland County, Virginia, a Cherokee–Shawnee party seeking revenge on Matthias Harman mistakenly hit Thomas Wiley’s cabin. Jenny (Virginia Sellards) Wiley saw her brother and three children killed; she and an infant were taken captive. Over nearly eleven months in the Big Sandy country the infant died and another child born in captivity was killed. She escaped by following a dream-path of creeks to Harman’s Station near present-day Paintsville, where men built a log raft and pulled her across. Reunited with Thomas in Virginia, the couple settled on Tom’s Creek in what became Johnson County in 1800; she died there in 1831. Jenny Wiley State Resort Park carries her name.
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/359

🏙️ Yellow Banks becomes Owensboro
Frontiersman William Smeathers (Bill Smothers) built a cabin on the south bank of the Ohio in 1797 at a landing flatboat men already called Yellow Banks for its clay bluffs. Daviess County was created in 1815; the town was laid out in 1816 and incorporated in 1817 as Owensborough for Col. Abraham Owen, killed at Tippecanoe in 1811 (the spelling shortened later). Smothers later scouted for Stephen F. Austin’s Old Three Hundred in Texas. Downtown Smothers Park marks the home site — western Kentucky’s river gate still sitting on that yellow bank.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owensboro,_Kentucky

🪶 Erie — the Cat Nation cleared from the lake shore
The Erie (Erielhénon, “people of the long tail”), called la Nation du Chat by the French, were an Iroquoian people of fortified maize villages along southern Lake Erie. In the Beaver Wars they sheltered Huron refugees and clashed with the Seneca; between about 1654 and 1656 Iroquois armies with Dutch firearms destroyed their towns. Survivors were adopted into Iroquois nations or scattered south and west. That collapse helped open the upper Ohio Valley as contested hunting ground — the same middle ground later claimed by Shawnee, Mingo, and Wyandot parties who wintered and raided south of the river into Kentucky.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie_people

🗿 Buckner Site — Fort Ancient villages on Strodes Creek
Near Paris in Bourbon County, the Buckner Site (15Bb12) holds two circular Fort Ancient villages around central plazas, occupied mainly about A.D. 1200–1400 and into the early 1500s. Wall-trench houses, shell- and limestone-tempered pottery, triangular points, and a stone-paved McCray Mound mortuary area show maize farmers tied into Ohio Valley exchange. University of Kentucky WPA crews dug there in 1939; later work mapped the rings. It was listed on the National Register in 1983 (address restricted) — Bluegrass country that was already a settled farming landscape centuries before Paris or Bourbon whiskey.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckner_site

🥃 Jacob Beam sells Old Jake’s first barrel
In 1795 Jacob Beam (born Johannes Jacob Boehm) sold his first barrel of “Old Jake Beam” sour-mash whiskey from a corn farm and mill on Hardin’s Creek in what was then Washington County, Kentucky. The family later grew the Old Tub brand; after Prohibition Jim Beam reopened the line, and seven generations still distill from that pioneer start. One early barrel sale became the Beam house that still marks Nelson County’s map.
Source: https://www.jimbeam.com/en-us/our-story/beam-history
