# Old Forester, Prestonsburg, and Moluntha

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Tuesday, October 13, 2026*

### 🤠 Col. Thomas Marshall at Buck Pond
Virginia-born Col. Thomas Marshall (1730–1802), father of Chief Justice John Marshall, commanded the 3rd Virginia Regiment at Brandywine and Germantown, wintered at Valley Forge, and was captured at Charleston in 1780. On parole he first rode into Kentucky and located Woodford County land; in 1783 Virginia made him surveyor general of the military lands owed Continental Line officers and soldiers, with an office in Lexington. He founded Buck Pond near today’s Versailles that same year; the Federal-style house was finished by 1785. Around 1800 he turned the farm over to his youngest son, Dr. Louis Marshall. Six generations of Marshalls lived under that Revolutionary grant. He sat for Fayette in the Virginia House of Delegates (1787–88), backed Kentucky’s separation conventions at Danville, and later died in Mason County—a surveyor’s station on Bluegrass limestone long before Buck Pond was a Thoroughbred nursery.

### 🏙️ Prestonsburg: first seat on the Big Sandy
Surveyor John Graham of Virginia laid out Preston’s Station in 1797 on an 800-acre tract tied to Col. John Preston’s grants along the Levisa Fork. On May 3, 1797, the plat became Prestonsburg—the first permanent town in eastern Kentucky’s Big Sandy Valley. When the Assembly created Floyd County (effective June 1, 1800) and named it for explorer John Floyd, Prestonsburg became the seat of a vast mountain county that later spun off many of today’s eastern counties. The first courthouse burned April 8, 1808, wiping early records; the town was formally established Jan. 2, 1818. Six residents made the 1800 census, yet the river fork seat still anchors Floyd County.

### 🥃 George Garvin Brown and America’s first bottled bourbon [BOURBON]
Munfordville-born George Garvin Brown (1846–1917) moved to Louisville, sold drugs wholesale, and in 1870 launched J. T. S. Brown & Bro. with half-brother J. T. S. Brown Jr. His answer to barrel-side watering was Old Forester: bourbon sold only in sealed glass bottles, batched for consistency from nearby Louisville distilleries, each bottle signed as his guarantee—at first about 90 proof. Company tradition ties the early “Forrester” spelling to physician Dr. William Forrester; the brand later dropped an “r.” It became the foundation of Brown-Forman (renamed 1890 for bookkeeper-partner George Forman). Old Forester is still sold by the same company before, during, and after Prohibition—one of six Kentucky medicinal whiskey licenses—and remains the longest continually bottled bourbon brand.

### 🪶 Moluntha and Logan’s Raid, 1786
Moluntha (d. 1786), also Melonthe or Malunthy, was the Mekoche Shawnee ceremonial “principal chief” who succeeded Kishshinottisthee after 1780—the spokesman Americans often mistook for a Shawnee king. In January 1786 he and other Mekoche leaders reluctantly signed the Treaty of Fort Finney, ceding most of Ohio; most Shawnees rejected it. That October Gen. Benjamin Logan’s Kentucky militia burned seven Shawnee towns north of the Ohio. Elderly Moluntha came out under an American flag holding the treaty as proof of friendship. Hugh McGary, still bitter over Blue Licks (1782), asked if he had been at that fight; Moluntha apparently misunderstood and said yes. McGary tomahawked and scalped him. A court-martial suspended McGary for a year. Historian Colin Calloway later wrote that real hope of Shawnee–American peace died with Moluntha—Tecumseh still cited the murder in 1810.
Source: [Moluntha (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moluntha)

### 🏺 Sassafras Ridge: Mississippian town on the western border
Near the Mississippi floodplain in Fulton County, Sassafras Ridge (15Fu3) was a Mississippian mound-and-village center—one of the western Kentucky border towns studied with Adams and Wickliffe. It once held three mounds (two later destroyed); ceramics include Matthews Incised, O’Byam Incised, and Barton Incised, with surface finds of negative-painted ware and a possible owl-effigy bottle fragment. Occupation belongs to the Mississippian centuries roughly AD 1000–1500 (not the 1978–80 survey years). Listed on the National Register in 1984 (address restricted), it marks farmers and ritual specialists on Kentucky’s far western edge centuries before Chickasaw title and the Jackson Purchase.
