Squire Boone's Painted Stone
Daniel's younger brother Squire Maugridge Boone Jr. carved his name into a Clear Creek rock, painted the letters red, and by spring 1780 planted Shelby County's first large station about two miles north of present Shelbyville. Thirteen families and a 23-man militia held Painted Stone through raids; after an April 1781 wound left Squire's right arm permanently short, most settlers fled toward Linn's Station and walked into the Long Run Massacre of September 13. He later rebuilt a mill on the burnt site before land debts cost him the station. Local ground for Bagdad and Shelby County.

Paris from Hopewell
Joseph Houston's 1776 station sat near Doyle Spring. Lawrence Protzman later platted 250 acres and in 1789 got Virginia to charter the town as Hopewell (for his New Jersey hometown) as Bourbon County's seat. In 1790 James Garrard and locals renamed it Paris to match the county, itself named for France's Bourbon kings who backed the Revolution. The post office briefly ran as Bourbonton before Paris stuck.

Capt. Will & the hunting ground
Shawnee held the larger northern and central Kentucky hunting grounds; Cherokee worked closer to Cumberland Gap. When long hunters stacked pelts for eastern markets, Native parties treated them as poachers. Around late 1769 a Shawnee band with Captain Will Emery (Cherokee-tied, often with Shawnee) took Boone and John Stewart's six-month haul and warned them home: stay out, or the wasps and yellowjackets would sting. Not blank wilderness. A contested commons.

Ashland's Adena mounds
Six conical Adena burial mounds still sit in Ashland's Central Park, roughly 15–20 feet across and 4–6 feet high in a slight curve. UK archaeologists Webb and Funkhouser recorded them in 1932; a 1930s survey said no Kentucky city held more prehistoric evidence than Ashland, though many other local mounds were leveled for streets and buildings. Listed on the National Register in 1974, they mark Woodland mound-builder presence along the Ohio centuries before historic tribes.

Christopher Gist, 1750–51
Fifteen-plus years before Boone's fame, the Ohio Company hired surveyor Christopher Gist to probe the Ohio Valley down toward the Falls. Crossing into Kentucky from the Scioto mouth, his journal logged rich bottomlands, canebrakes, buffalo plains, and Native towns. Shawnee at Lower Shawneetown warned him off the Falls, where French-allied Indians camped. He brought the company a fossil "beest" tooth from Big Bone country and the first detailed English read of northeastern Kentucky.
