🤠 Capt. William Hubbell’s Ohio Flatboat Stand (1791)
On March 23, 1791, Capt. William Hubbell of the Frankfort frontier was taking a flatboat down the Ohio toward Limestone (today’s Maysville) with Daniel Light, William Plascut’s family, and others—about twenty souls after late pickups. At dawn near the Kanawha country, three Indian canoes closed in. Hubbell’s nine armed men held a running fight; he kept firing after a ball wrecked his gun lock, used a firebrand to touch off another shot, then drove boarders back with pistols and firewood even after a bullet tore his right arm. Two of his men died in the boat, another later at Limestone; the women and children survived. The party reached Limestone that night. A companion boat under Capt. Greathouse, which had not prepared to fight, was taken ashore with grim results.

🏙️ Augusta on the Ohio (1797)
Captain Philip Buckner, a Revolutionary War commissary officer with Virginia land grants in what became Bracken County, gave 600 acres to lay out Augusta in 1797. The Kentucky legislature chartered the river town that October; Augusta served as Bracken’s early county seat. Buckner later sat in the 1799 constitutional convention and the legislature, and lived many years at Woodlawn before dying in 1830. Augusta’s Ohio-River landing put northeastern Kentucky on a main corridor between Limestone and Cincinnati long before Covington filled the Licking Point.
Source: https://history.ky.gov/markers/founder-of-augusta

🏹 Egushawa and the Odawa in Kentucky’s War Years
Egushawa (also Au-goosh-away), the Detroit-area Odawa war chief often treated as Pontiac’s successor, helped Britain recruit Wabash and Great Lakes allies against Kentucky settlements. In 1780 he led an Ottawa contingent with Capt. Henry Bird’s cannon-backed invasion that forced the surrenders at Ruddell’s and Martin’s stations and marched hundreds of prisoners north toward Detroit. After 1783 he rejected land cessions made without tribal consent, fought in the Northwest Confederacy’s victories over Harmar and St. Clair, was wounded at Fallen Timbers in 1794, and signed Greenville in 1795 as a principal Ottawa spokesman—tying Odawa strategy on the Maumee and Detroit to the raids that shaped Kentucky’s last Revolutionary decade.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egushawa

🏛️ Cloudsplitter Rockshelter (Menifee County)
In the Red River Gorge sandstone country, Cloudsplitter Rockshelter holds a deep Archaic-to-Early Woodland record. Dry sand preserved a roughly 3,700-year-old squash rind—early evidence that people here were tending plants—as well as chipped and ground stone tools and pollen that tracks climate change. Later Woodland layers at Cloudsplitter and neighboring Gorge shelters (Newt Kash, Haystack, Rogers) show storage pits, seed crops such as sunflower, sumpweed, maygrass, and knotweed, and a shift from nut-heavy foraging toward small-scale gardens. The Gorge’s dry shelters are why eastern Kentucky figures so largely in the story of plant domestication in eastern North America.
Source: https://www.livingarchaeologyweekend.org/_files/ugd/facae2_6e9670882c8d4fab7707d41bb9aeb65b.pdf

⚕️ Ephraim McDowell & Jane Todd Crawford (1809)
On Christmas Day 1809 in Danville, Dr. Ephraim McDowell—Scotch-Irish, Edinburgh-trained, practicing on the Kentucky frontier since 1795—removed a 22.5-pound ovarian tumor from Jane Todd Crawford of Greensburg. She had ridden roughly sixty miles believing she was overdue in pregnancy; McDowell diagnosed a cyst and operated in his home without anesthesia or antisepsis while assistants held her. The surgery lasted about twenty-five minutes. Crawford recovered and lived more than three decades more. McDowell’s later reports helped prove that opening the abdomen need not be a death sentence, making Danville a landmark in the history of surgery west of the Alleghenies.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim_McDowell
