🤠 Harry Innes
Virginia lawyer Harry Innes reached Kentucky in 1783 after Virginia elected him an assistant judge of the District of Kentucky’s supreme court; he was sworn near present-day Danville in November 1782. He served as attorney general for the Kentucky District (1784–1789), sat in eight of the ten statehood conventions that separated Kentucky from Virginia, and on September 26, 1789, became the first U.S. district judge for Kentucky—appointed by George Washington—holding that seat until his death in Frankfort in 1816. He farmed and kept a library on Elkhorn Creek, hosted figures from Isaac Shelby to Aaron Burr, and later endured bitter Spanish-conspiracy libel fights with Humphrey Marshall. Frankfort Cemetery holds his grave; Innes County, Mississippi, carries his name.

🏙️ Hopkinsville
Bartholomew Wood claimed a Christian County tract in 1796 and, with his wife Martha Ann, donated five acres and a half interest in Old Rock Spring when the new county seated justice there in 1797. A log courthouse and jail rose by 1798; locals tried the name Elizabeth for the Woods’ eldest daughter, but Elizabethtown already held that name. In 1804 the General Assembly established the town as Hopkinsville for Gen. Samuel Hopkins—Revolutionary veteran, Transylvania Company agent, and later Henderson County organizer—whose name also marks Hopkins County. It remains Christian County’s seat.

🐎 Phosphatic limestone and horse country
Inner Bluegrass soils rest on phosphatic Lexington and Cynthiana limestones, with high calcium and phosphorus. University of Kentucky soil tests still show that edge over the rest of the state—about two-thirds of Bluegrass samples testing high for phosphorus in a late twentieth-century lab series. After the Revolution, from about 1780 to 1820, Virginia and Carolina horse-raising families pushed west onto that forage-rich ground just as industrial America needed more horsepower. UK equine soil scientist Joshua McGrath argues the Horse Capital story starts with geology and settlement timing—high soil phosphorus supporting forage yields before commercial fertilizer—not a magic bone myth alone.

🏛️ Twin Mounds Site
Near Barlow in Ballard County, just north of where the Ohio meets the Mississippi, Twin Mounds (15Ba2 / Nolan; village 15Ba14) was a Mississippian administrative center with two platform mounds around a plaza and a village midden up to about two meters thick. Principal occupation ran about 1200–1450 CE (Dorena and Medley phases). It stood contemporary with Wickliffe Mounds a few miles southeast; as Wickliffe thinned around 1300, people appear to have shifted toward Twin Mounds. Excavations recovered a rare Cahokia-style flint-clay human effigy pipe carved from Missouri pipestone.

🪶 Nonhelema
Nonhelema Hokolesqua (active mid-1700s–1786), sister of the Shawnee leader Cornstalk, was a Chalakatha (Chillicothe) chief, diplomat, and warrior whom settlers nicknamed “the Grenadier” for her height. By mid-century she held influence at Lower Shawneetown on the Ohio–Scioto confluence—Kentucky’s northeastern corner across from the main town—and later warned American posts of British-allied raids even after Cornstalk’s 1777 murder at Fort Randolph. In 1785 Congress answered her land petition with a ration pension instead of Ohio acreage. In 1786 Kentucky militia under Benjamin Logan struck her Ohio village; her husband Moluntha was killed (by Hugh McGary in the usual accounts), and she died later that year after captivity.
