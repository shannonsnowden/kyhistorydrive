🤠 Richard Henderson
North Carolina judge and land speculator Richard Henderson reorganized his investors as the Transylvania Company in early 1775, then bargained at Sycamore Shoals (today’s Elizabethton, Tennessee) for roughly 20 million acres between the Kentucky, Cumberland, and Ohio rivers. Cherokee headmen including Attakullakulla and Oconostota signed in mid-March for about £10,000 in goods; warrior Dragging Canoe opposed the sale and warned the country would become dark and bloody. Virginia later voided the private purchase but compensated the company with Ohio River acreage that seeded Henderson County.

🏙️ Bardstown
The Bardstown area was explored in the mid-1770s and briefly called Salem. In 1780 William Bard arrived as agent for his brother David and John Cockey Owings, surveyed a town on their 1,000-acre grant, and set aside ground for public buildings. Named seat of new Nelson County in 1784, it was chartered by Virginia as Baird’s Town in 1788 and settled into the name Bardstown—one of Kentucky’s oldest inland towns, still county seat on the Beech Fork watershed.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardstown,_Kentucky

🪶 Treaty of Fort Stanwix (1768)
At Fort Stanwix (Rome, New York), Sir William Johnson and the Haudenosaunee Six Nations drew a new Line of Property that pushed the Proclamation of 1763 boundary west to the Ohio, then downriver toward the Tennessee—effectively treating most of Kentucky as Iroquois-ceded hunting ground sold to the Crown for a large payment in goods. Shawnee and Lenape peoples who actually used those grounds were present but not signatories and rejected the right of distant Iroquois councils to sell their homeland. The disputed cession helped set the fuse for Dunmore’s War and later Ohio Valley fighting.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Fort_Stanwix

🏛️ Fox Farm (Mason County)
On a broad ridgetop near Mays Lick, Fort Ancient farmers lived at Fox Farm (15Ms1) from about 1300 into the mid-1600s—one of the longest-lived villages of its kind in the Ohio Valley. Houses first ringed a plaza, then clustered around a large public building as population grew and floor plans stretched toward sixty feet. Residents planted corn, beans, and squash on the uplands, managed turkey harvests toward adult males to keep local flocks viable, and once curated eleven deer jaws as a ritual offering inside a house wall.
https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Fox-Farm.aspx

⛰️ Thomas Walker and Cumberland Gap
In March 1750 Dr. Thomas Walker led a Loyal Land Company party through the Appalachians and on April 13 recorded Cave Gap: a plain Indian road, a spring pouring from a cave, marked trees, and a pass visible for miles. He named the gap and nearby river for the Duke of Cumberland, noted coal on Flat Creek, and later had a small cabin raised near present Barbourville—the first Euro-American house claimed in Kentucky. Walker’s journal mapped the geologic doorway later generations would pour through.
