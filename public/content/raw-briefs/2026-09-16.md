*Kentucky History Morning Update — Wednesday, September 16, 2026*

🤠 Maj. Joseph Bowman
Virginia-born Joseph Bowman came to Kentucky by 1775, signed the 1776 Harrodsburg county petition, and in 1778 raised a company for George Rogers Clark. As Clark’s second-in-command he took Prairie du Rocher, St. Philippe, and Cahokia after Kaskaskia fell, kept a day-by-day journal of the flooded winter march to Vincennes, and was promoted major just as a celebratory powder explosion burned him badly. He died at Fort Patrick Henry on about August 14–15, 1779 — the only American officer lost in the Illinois campaign.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bowman

🏙 Newport at the Licking Mouth
Hubbard Taylor platted the Ohio–Licking point for the family; James Taylor Jr. settled in 1793, pushed Campbell County through in 1794, and on December 14, 1795 the General Assembly chartered Newport on Taylor land. He named it for Admiral Christopher Newport of Jamestown fame, reserved a public square and riverfront common, and in 1803 sold the army five acres at the Licking mouth for what became Newport Barracks — the post that locked the town into the river trade.
Source: https://newportky.gov/268/History

🪶 Illinois Confederation at Cahokia
After Clark seized the Illinois Country posts in July 1778, he called tribal leaders to Cahokia and offered peace or war. Bands of the Illinois Confederation (Illiniwek) — Kaskaskia, Cahokia, Peoria, Michigamea, and others — largely chose peace; Kaskaskia leader Jean-Baptiste Ducoigne became a working American ally with hunters and scouts. That middle-ground diplomacy cut British leverage over some Mississippi–Wabash nations and was meant to blunt the Detroit-directed raids that had hammered Kentucky stations.
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/Illinois_campaign

🏺 Adams Site (15Fu4)
Near Hickman in Fulton County, on Bayou de Chien toward the Mississippi, the Adams Site is a Mississippian town built over an earlier Late Woodland village. Platform mounds around a central plaza (plus a smaller plaza southwest), deep village middens up to about 1–1.5 meters thick, and occupation roughly 1100–1500 CE place it with sister border towns like Wickliffe and Sassafras Ridge. It went on the National Register in 1984 for what it can still teach about western Kentucky’s late prehistoric farmers.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams_site

🧂 Drennon Springs Salt Works
In Henry County — a short drive from Shelby/Bagdad — Jacob Drennon and Matthew Bracken claimed the big salt lick on July 26, 1773; Nicholas Cresswell in 1775 described some fifty acres of buffalo-trampled ground and springs strong enough to leave salt crusts in the sun. Virginia’s Patrick Henry patented 400 acres including the springs to George Rogers Clark in 1785, and boiling works rose that winter. 1990s bridge archaeology documented ten stone-lined furnace trenches and rare kettle fragments — one of the few early Kentucky salt industries dug with care.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Drennon-Springs.aspx
