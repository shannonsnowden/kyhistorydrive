White Oak Springs to Red Banks

*Kentucky History Morning Update — Saturday, September 19, 2026*

🤠 Capt. Nathaniel Hart
Capt. Nathaniel Hart (1734–1782), a Transylvania Company proprietor and chief negotiator with the Cherokee, came to Boonesborough in 1775 and in 1779 settled White Oak Springs (Hart's Station) about a mile from the fort, joined by Dutch families from Pennsylvania. The log house tied to his claim is still cited as Madison County's oldest standing house; he was killed nearby in 1782. His daughter Susannah later married Isaac Shelby, Kentucky's first and fifth governor.
Source: https://history.ky.gov/markers/home-of-capt-nathaniel-hart

🏙 Henderson at Red Banks
The Ohio River bluff known as Red Banks sat on the Virginia grant later confirmed to Richard Henderson & Company. On April 6, 1797, Gen. Samuel Hopkins and surveyor Thomas Allin finished laying out the town of Henderson—named for the company founder, who had died in 1785. That plan is still treated as the city's birthday; Henderson County followed in 1798–99.
Source: https://hendersonky.gov/141/History-of-Henderson

🪶 Ojibwe on Bird's War Road
In Capt. Henry Bird's British invasion of Kentucky in June 1780, roughly a hundred Odawa and Ojibwe (Chippewa) warriors left Detroit with Bird's guns before hundreds of Shawnee joined on the Great Miami. The combined force took Ruddle's and Martin's Stations on the Licking with artillery—the deepest British-and-Indigenous strike into the Kentucky settlements that summer—and marched hundreds of captives toward Detroit.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird%27s_invasion_of_Kentucky

🏺 Turk Site, Carlisle County
On a bluff spur near Bardwell overlooking the Mississippi floodplain, the Turk Site (15Ce6) was a small Mississippian regional center of about 2.5 hectares. Occupied mainly in the Dorena phase (about 1100–1300 CE) and into the Medley phase, it arranged platform mounds around a plaza—tightened by the spur's narrow geography—and may have drawn people from the nearby older Marshall Site. Richard Edging's 1980s work remains the key modern investigation.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turk_Site

🌊 Lost River Cave
Under Bowling Green, Lost River Cave is a Pennyroyal karst system cut in Mississippian St. Louis and Ste. Genevieve limestones: a surface stream sinks into roughly seven miles of passages draining a large underground watershed. Settlers harnessed the flow for a grist mill at the cave mouth by the late 1700s; today the preserve offers Kentucky's only underground boat tour through the same vanished river.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_River_Cave
