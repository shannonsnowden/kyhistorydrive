🤠 Hugh McGary, the Blue Licks Hothead
Irish-born Hugh McGary (1744–1806) came into Kentucky in 1775 with Boone's party, settled near Fort Harrod, and built McGary's Station around 1779 at Shawnee Springs in today's Mercer County. On August 19, 1782, as a major under Col. John Todd, he commanded the center at Blue Licks. When Boone warned of an ambush at the Licking Ford, McGary rode across shouting for anyone who wasn't a coward to follow. The militia plunged into the trap; roughly seventy Kentuckians fell in minutes, including Todd and Israel Boone. McGary survived—and later killed the surrendered Shawnee chief Moluntha on Logan's 1786 Ohio raid, earning a court-martial suspension. He remains Kentucky's classic frontier hothead: brave, quarrelsome, and impossible to ignore.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_McGary

🏙️ Bagdad: How a Shelby County Rail Stop Took Over Consolation
Northeastern Shelby County's Bagdad grew because the Louisville and Frankfort Railroad reached the area about 1850. Earlier settlers clustered at Consolation, roughly a mile east, with a post office from 1849; once the depot and mill sat on the line, people drifted west and Consolation faded. On October 12, 1861, the Christiansburg post office moved to the station and took the name Bagdad, with Richard Radford as first postmaster. The name's origin is still unsettled—miller's-son folklore, a nickname for Radford, or something else—but the place became a shipping point for flour, meal, livestock, and tobacco. Bagdad still sits at KY 12 and 395, about eleven miles northeast of Shelbyville, at the headwaters of Sixmile and Guist Creeks.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagdad,_Kentucky

🪶 Wyandot Name: Kah-ten-tah-teh, Fair Land of Tomorrow
Long before Filson popularized Dark and Bloody Ground, several nations had their own words for this country. Forest Service and place-name studies record that Wyandot hunters coming south of the Ohio—often along the Warriors' Path—called it Kah-ten-tah-teh, rendered Fair Land of Tomorrow or Land Where We Will Live Tomorrow. Shawnee forms such as Kain-tuck-ee (often glossed at the head of the river) and Iroquoian meadow/level roots also circulated among early traders. Kentucky's English name is still debated; what matters on the ground is that Wyandot, Shawnee, Delaware, and others treated the Bluegrass as named, claimed hunting country—not empty space—before stations rose south of the Ohio.
Source: https://www.npshistory.com/publications/usfs/region/8/daniel-boone/history/appa.htm

🏛️ Muir Site: Early Fort Ancient on a Jessamine Ridge
Before the Nicholasville Bypass went in, University of Kentucky archaeologists dug the Muir site (15Js86) on a broad upland ridge west of Nicholasville. Radiocarbon and ceramics place it in the early Fort Ancient Osborne Phase, roughly 1010–1255 CE. Unlike later crowded plaza villages, Muir held only one to three rectangular single-set-post houses at a time, each with a shallow floor basin and central hearth—more a dispersed farmstead cluster than a stockaded town. Residents ate mostly deer, elk, and bear, grew corn, beans, knotweed, and sunflower, and made limestone-tempered jars with rare guilloche designs. Long, eared triangular points set Muir's flintwork apart from some northern Kentucky Fort Ancient neighbors.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Muir.aspx

🏛️ Cleek-McCabe: A Circular Fort Ancient Village in Boone County
On the Mud Lick Creek floodplain near Walton, Cleek-McCabe is a thirteenth-century Fort Ancient village: a ~100-foot-wide midden ring around a ~200-foot plaza, with low burial mounds east and west. UK crews in the late 1930s sampled the ring and dug the western mound; the eastern mound still stands. Under that western mound they found three successive roofless public buildings—first a 43-foot circle, then rectangles up to about 56 by 95 feet—sharing a central pole pit that links Fort Ancient ritual architecture to earlier Adena and Hopewell submound screens. Cordmarked jars with guilloche necks and triangular strap handles tie the pottery to Boone County and neighboring Ohio–Indiana Fort Ancient communities.
Source: https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Cleek-McCabe.aspx
