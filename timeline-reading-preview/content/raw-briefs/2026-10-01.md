🤠 James Knox
Before Boone’s fame crowded the memory, long hunter Col. James Knox led a party of Virginia and Carolina hunters through Cumberland Gap into Kentucky. The 1942 Historic Camp Knox marker says that in 1770 he settled here with 22 men, who cured and stored 2,300 deer hides and other furs in a skin house; Green County’s history page says the hunters named the branch beside the camp Skinhouse Branch. Kentucky Historical Society marker 709 says Knox led 40 Long Hunters to Kentucky in 1770, built a camp with nine men in what is now Green County, and returned to Virginia in 1771. Sources differ on dates: an 1892 biography has the party crossing the Gap in 1769 and puts the Green River skin camp in 1774, so this story follows the markers’ 1770–1771. The 1892 biography says the Cherokee leader Captain Dick directed Knox’s men to a stream they named Dick’s (now Dix) River, and Wikipedia’s long-hunter article says Knox named it for him. Knox later recruited a company for Virginia’s 8th Regiment, commanded a company in Morgan’s Rifles at Saratoga (8th Virginia Regiment site), and served in the Kentucky Senate from Lincoln County, 1795–1800 (marker 709).

🏙️ Somerset
Pulaski County needed a seat after its 1799 creation from Lincoln and Green. In 1801 the court laid out Somerset on forty acres donated by William Dodson, beside a dependable spring whose path became the town’s busiest early street. Migrants from Somerset County, New Jersey, gave the place its name. Kentucky Historical Society Marker 1607 still marks the Old Town Spring—where locals joked that whoever drank would gain wisdom and always return.

🪶 Moneton
The Moneton (“Big Water” people) lived along the Kanawha and New Rivers in the middle Ohio Valley—on Kentucky’s eastern approaches—when English explorers first wrote them down. Thomas Batts noted them in 1671; Gabriel Arthur, sent by Abraham Wood from Fort Henry, described their “great town” in 1674. Likely tied to Fort Ancient networks and sometimes linked to Siouan neighbors, they vanish from the record soon after, absorbed under disease, Iroquois pressure, and the slave trade—many scholars argue into Shawnee communities—before Kentucky stations rose west of their homeland.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moneton

🏺 Croley-Evans Site
On the Upper Cumberland in Knox County, Croley-Evans (15KX24) was a Mississippian town-and-mound complex. A 1996 study by UK archaeologist Richard Jefferies and colleagues reports that its most intensive Mississippian occupation dates from about A.D. 1200 to 1450 by radiocarbon. The authors find that Upper Cumberland groups shared many traits with “mainstream” Mississippian groups to the south and west, but expressed them in ways that reflect the region’s peripheral location in the Southeast. The site was listed on the National Register in 1985 (address restricted).

⛏️ Mammoth Cave saltpeter
Long before tourism, Mammoth Cave’s nitrate-rich dirt fed gunpowder. In the War of 1812, when British blockade cut imported saltpeter, operators worked the Rotunda and nearby passages at industrial scale—leaching cave soil in wooden vats, pumping liquor to the surface, and converting it to potassium nitrate. Enslaved laborers did most of the hauling and filtering; remnants of vats, pipes, and pump works still stand just inside the Historic Entrance. After the war, when prices fell, the workings were abandoned and the cave became a minor tourist attraction.
