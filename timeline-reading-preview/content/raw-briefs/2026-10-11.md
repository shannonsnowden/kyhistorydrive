🤠 Simon Girty, the frontier’s most hated interpreter
Simon Girty was born in 1741 at Chambers’ Mills in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. In 1756, during the French and Indian War, he and his brothers James and George were captured and carried to Kittanning, and the boys were divided up: Simon went to the Seneca, James to the Shawnee, and George to the Delaware. Simon was adopted by a Seneca family, became fluent in Seneca, and learned several other Native languages, which made him valuable for years afterward as an interpreter and hunter around Fort Pitt. Disillusioned with the Patriot cause, he left Pittsburgh in March 1778 with Alexander McKee and reached Detroit in April, where Lt. Gov. Henry Hamilton hired him as an interpreter for the British Indian Department. Kentuckians knew him best from August 1782, when he, McKee, and Matthew Elliott came south with Capt. William Caldwell’s Butler’s Rangers and about 300 Shawnee warriors under Blue Jacket to attack Bryan Station, east of present Lexington. Kentucky tradition remembers Girty calling on the fort to surrender; the stockade held, and Caldwell lifted the siege after two days. Colonel John Todd’s militia pursued, and on August 19 Caldwell’s force, waiting in the ravines at the Blue Licks, killed 77 Kentuckians, Todd among them. Girty stayed on the Indian Department payroll as an interpreter after the war, retired in 1795, and died in 1818 on land the British had granted him at the mouth of the Detroit River in Upper Canada. The map pin is the Bryan Station siege site, which has its own story here.

🏙️ Owingsville: a town named on a wager
In 1795 Colonel Thomas Dye Owings was sent from Maryland to Kentucky by his father to operate some of the first iron furnaces in the region. Within fifteen years he had amassed a good deal of wealth and land. Owings and Colonel Richard H. Menefee each owned large parcels of the ground where the town would stand, and, as the story is told, the two men wagered that whoever built the finer home the quickest would give the community his name. Owings won, and Owingsville was founded in 1811, the year Bath County was formed. It is the county seat, set roughly at the center of the county where US 60 meets KY 36. The map pin marks the town.

🧂 Mann’s Lick, Louisville’s forgotten salt town
Mann’s Lick was a salt lick just north of the present-day Fairdale neighborhood of Louisville. It was named for John Mann, a member of Captain Thomas Bullitt’s surveying party of 1773. The land was given to Colonel John Todd in 1780, and when Todd died at the Battle of Blue Licks in 1782 it passed to his daughter, Mary O. Todd. Joseph Brooks, a Pennsylvanian who had worked at Bullitt’s Lick near Shepherdsville, leased it from her in 1787 and started a saltworks at once, building a hollow-log pipe to carry brine to his furnace; remains of the logs could still be seen in the 1940s. Fear of Indian attack led to fortifications around the works in 1788. Hundreds of workers, many of them enslaved, made the salt, and some settled on James F. Moore’s land nearby, leading to the 1794 charter of Newtown. Sold under brands such as Little Sandy Salt, it was prized by merchants and sold as far away as Lexington; in 1808 it fetched $2 to $2.25 a bushel, and at its height the works ran around the clock. But the lick was smaller than Bullitt’s, and steamboats brought cheaper salt to Louisville; by 1830 the town and the salt had dwindled. Newcomers after 1910 renamed the area Fairdale, and Manslick Road keeps the old name. The map pin is an approximate point just north of Fairdale.

🪶 Little Turtle and Kentucky’s 1790 militia disaster
Little Turtle, Mihšihkinaahkwa in the Miami language, was a Miami war leader who had lived at Turtletown on the Eel River. In the fall of 1790 Gen. Josiah Harmar marched north from Fort Washington, at present Cincinnati, against the Miami towns at Kekionga, present Fort Wayne, Indiana, with 320 regulars and 1,133 militia from Kentucky and Pennsylvania. On October 19 a patrol under Col. John Hardin, the Kentucky militia leader whose own story is told here, chased a lone rider who proved to be a decoy into a swampy meadow by the Eel River, about 13 miles northwest of Kekionga, where the warriors opened fire; among the dead was the son of Kentucky militia leader Charles Scott. The fight is sometimes called Hardin’s Defeat, and more costly clashes near Kekionga followed on October 22. In November 1791 the confederacy Little Turtle helped lead destroyed Gen. Arthur St. Clair’s army. When he could not persuade the confederacy to negotiate, he stepped down as war chief; after Fallen Timbers he went to Greenville and spoke before signing the treaty of 1795. The 1790 battles were fought in present Indiana, so this story has no map pin.

🪨 Red River Gorge: sandstone arches and the dam fight
The Red River Gorge is a canyon system on the Red River in east-central Kentucky, part of the Pottsville Escarpment, carved over millions of years by erosion and weathering into high sandstone cliffs, rock shelters, waterfalls, and natural bridges. More than 100 natural sandstone arches stand in the gorge, the highest concentration of arches anywhere east of the Mississippi, and its rock shelters hold evidence of people from the Paleoindian period on, including early plant domestication. In 1962 the “Great Flood of Clay City,” the worst in 102 years, led officials and local communities to press the state and the Kennedy administration for a flood-control dam, and the Army Corps of Engineers began planning one. Many people feared the reservoir would destroy the gorge’s unusual ecosystem, and the Sierra Club led the opposition. The struggle lasted decades and involved two proposed dam sites. The gorge became a National Natural Landmark and National Archaeological District in 1976, and the fight ended on December 3, 1993, when President Bill Clinton signed the Red River Designation Act, adding the river to the National Wild and Scenic Rivers system and barring any dam. Today the roughly 29,000-acre Red River Gorge Geological Area lies within the Daniel Boone National Forest, beside Natural Bridge State Park. The map pin is Wikipedia’s point for the gorge, in Menifee County; the geological area also reaches into Powell and Wolfe counties.
