*Kentucky History Morning Update — Sunday, September 13, 2026*

🤠 Col. Stephen Trigg of Viney Grove
Virginia-born Stephen Trigg (c. 1744–1782) signed the Fincastle Resolutions, sat on Virginia’s land court that sorted Kentucky claims in 1779–80, then settled Trigg’s Station (Viney Grove / Haggin’s Station) about four miles northeast of Harrodsburg on Cane Run. As Lincoln County’s militia colonel he helped push the 1780 act that laid out Louisville. On August 19, 1782 he commanded the right wing at Blue Licks and was killed in the ambush; Trigg County (1820) still carries his name, with a marker on the Cadiz courthouse lawn.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Trigg

🏙️ Covington at the Point
At the Ohio–Licking confluence—“the Point”—Thomas Kennedy ran a farm, stone tavern, and ferry (Kennedy’s Ferry) from about 1790. In 1814 John and Richard Gano with Thomas Carneal bought 150 acres from him for $50,000, platted streets to line up with Cincinnati across the river, and named the venture for War of 1812 Gen. Leonard Covington. The General Assembly chartered Covington on February 8, 1815; early north–south streets honored Kentucky’s first five governors. Lot sales crawled for years—only about 715 people by 1830—until German immigration and river commerce turned it into Northern Kentucky’s big city.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covington,_Kentucky

🪶 Wea (Waayaahtanwa) on the Wabash
The Wea—“people of the whirlpool”—were a Miami–Illinois-speaking people whose main towns clustered on the middle Wabash (Ouiatenon near today’s Lafayette, and villages toward Terre Haute) after Beaver Wars pressure eased and Miami bands returned west of the Appalachians. French Fort Ouiatenon (1717) traded with them; in the Revolution they generally sided with the British alongside Miami kin, then signed Greenville (1795) and a string of Wabash cessions (Grouseland 1805, Fort Wayne/Vincennes 1809, St. Mary’s 1818). That river corridor sat opposite western Kentucky’s Ohio shore, so Wea–Miami hunting and war parties were part of the same frontier pressure that hit Kentucky stations—distinct from the related Piankashaw already covered this month.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wea

🏺 Ward Site (15McL11), McLean County
On the Godfrey Ward farm along the Green River, WPA crews under John B. Elliott dug about 16,000 square feet in 1938 and catalogued 433 human burials plus dog burials and dozens of features—another Late Archaic shell-midden cemetery in the same Green River series as Indian Knoll and Carlston Annis. Later paleodemography treated Ward as a year-round fisher–hunter–gatherer community with heavy mussel, fish, deer, and nut use, plus clear skeletal evidence of trauma and short adult lifespans. With Butterfield and Barrett nearby, Ward helps pin the eastern edge of Kentucky’s densest Archaic shell-midden landscape.
Source: https://heritage.ky.gov/Documents/Green-River-Archaic.pdf

🕳️ Carter Caves & Cascade Falls
In north-central Carter County, more than twenty caverns honeycomb Mississippian limestone along Tygarts Creek. Cascade Cave—added to the state park in 1959 after private tours dating to the 1920s (and guiding lore back to the 1870s)—shows dripstone rooms, a lake chamber, and a roughly thirty-foot underground waterfall. Above ground, Smokey Bridge is often called Kentucky’s largest natural bridge, and a paved road still crosses Carter Cave Natural Bridge. Locals donated about 945 acres in 1946 to start Carter Caves State Resort Park, eastern Kentucky’s big public window on karst country outside Mammoth Cave.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carter_Caves_State_Resort_Park
