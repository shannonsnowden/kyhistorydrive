🤠 Bland W. Ballard of Tick Creek
Born Spotsylvania County, Va., October 16, 1761, Bland Williams Ballard came to Kentucky in 1779 and scouted for George Rogers Clark on the 1780 Ohio campaign and the 1786 Wabash expedition. With Robert Tyler he helped plant Tyler Station on Tick Creek east of Shelbyville around 1781. In 1788 Delaware warriors killed his father and several siblings at the family cabin near the station; Ballard fought through Fallen Timbers (1794), survived capture at Frenchtown (1813), represented Shelby County in the General Assembly, and died September 5, 1853 — 173 years ago today. Ballard County and Blandville carry his name; he rests in Frankfort Cemetery.

🏙️ Richmond from Miller's Barn
Madison County's first seat sat at hillside Milford with thin water. In 1798 the legislature let the court move onto Revolutionary War veteran Col. John Miller's land along Otter Creek; Milford partisans fought the change (local lore still names Dave Kennedy vs. William Kearly), and the county paid angry residents before carting records to Miller's barn. On July 4, 1798, trustees named the new seat Richmond for Miller's Virginia birthplace, surveyed fifty acres into lots, and built Tyra Rodes's brick courthouse on the barn site in 1799. Richmond incorporated in 1809.

🪶 Yuchi on the Green River maps
John R. Swanton's federal tribal handbook notes that early colonial maps place a Yuchi (Euchee) town on a Kentucky river matching the Green. The Yuchi were a language-isolate people of the southern Appalachians and Tennessee Valley who used the Ohio country more as hunting range than permanent farm country; the Green River map mark is one of the few named Yuchi footprints inside present Kentucky, alongside Cherokee claims on the upper Cumberland and Chickasaw claims in the far west.
https://accessgenealogy.com/kentucky/kentucky-indian-tribes.htm

🏛️ Gaitskill Mound's engraved tablets
On Mount Sterling's northern bypass, Gaitskill Mound is an Adena earthwork (roughly 800 B.C.–A.D. 700) listed on the National Register in 1975. Unlike many looted mounds, it yielded rare engraved stone and clay tablets recovered through University of Kentucky work associated with W.S. Webb — ritual objects tied to Adena cremation and mound ceremony rather than everyday trash. The site anchors Montgomery County's deep Woodland past beneath the later townscape.
https://history.ky.gov/markers/the-gaitskill-mound

🥃 James Crow on Glenn's Creek
Scottish physician-chemist James C. Crow began advising Oscar Pepper's Woodford County farm still on Glenn's Creek by 1833. After Pepper rebuilt in stone (1837–38), Crow returned as master distiller (1840–55), measuring mash temperatures, yeast, and acidity with scientific care that fixed sour-mash practice as commercial habit. His whiskey shipped far beyond Kentucky; Old Crow later took his name, and the limestone valley still hosts Woodford Reserve on the old Pepper ground.
