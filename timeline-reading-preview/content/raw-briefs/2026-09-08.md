🤠 Col. John Bowman at Old Chillicothe
In late May 1779, Col. John Bowman—Kentucky County’s first county-lieutenant—led roughly 160–300 mounted militia (with Benjamin Logan and Levi Todd) against the Shawnee town of Old Chillicothe near today’s Xenia, Ohio. They burned dozens of cabins and cornfields and drove off about 163 horses, but could not take the warriors’ blockhouse. Chief Blackfish was mortally wounded. Contemporaries first called it a failure; later voices, including Theodore Roosevelt, ranked the raid as strategically valuable for discouraging further Shawnee war parties into Kentucky.

🏙️ Elizabethtown from Severns Valley
Severns Valley took its name from early explorer John Severns. Permanent settlement began in spring 1780 when Capt. Thomas Helm, Col. Andrew Hynes, and Samuel Haycraft built three forts in a defensive triangle. Hynes surveyed thirty acres in 1793, donated the town site, and on July 4, 1797, the place was established as Elizabethtown for his wife Elizabeth—soon Hardin County’s seat.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethtown,_Kentucky

🪶 Kickapoo on the Kentucky frontier
Algonquian Kickapoo lived in villages along the Wabash River and its tributaries north of the Ohio, in what is now western Indiana. Indiana’s Department of Natural Resources says Kickapoo and Mascouten groups moved from Illinois into west-central Indiana in the 1730s and 1740s. None of the sources cited here places a Kickapoo village in Kentucky or documents Kickapoo raids on Kentucky stations. What they do show is Kentucky’s reach into Kickapoo country: in June 1791 volunteers under Brig. Gen. Charles Scott burned towns on the Wabash, and Scott’s report names a Kickapoo town on the river that his men attacked. The Kickapoo were named as a party to the 1795 Treaty of Greenville, and the 1805 Grouseland treaty with Delaware, Potawatomi, Miami, Eel River and Wea leaders preserved any Kickapoo claim to the country they occupied on the Vermillion River.

🏛️ Jonathan Creek Mississippian center
On the west bank of the Tennessee River in Marshall County (site 15Ml4), Jonathan Creek was a fortified Mississippian town occupied roughly A.D. 1200–1300. CCC crews working with the University of Kentucky excavated in 1940–42 before Kentucky Lake rose, recording platform and burial mounds, a plaza, multiple stockade lines with bastions, and remnants of some 70–89 houses in both single-post and wall-trench styles—evidence of a community built for defense and ritual.
https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Jonathan-Creek.aspx

📔 William Calk’s 1775 journal
From March 13 to May 2, 1775, William Calk kept a plainspoken daybook of his trek from Prince William County, Virginia, to Boone’s Fort on the Kentucky River. The manuscript—held by the Kentucky Historical Society—records mud, cold, lost livestock, meetings with Henderson’s Transylvania party, and the scramble to claim land. Calk later settled near Hinkston Creek and Mount Sterling; his diary remains one of the clearest settler voices from the first season of organized immigration.
