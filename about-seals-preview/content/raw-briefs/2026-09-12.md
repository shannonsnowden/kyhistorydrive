*Kentucky History Morning Update — Saturday, September 12, 2026*

🤠 Henry Skaggs, Green River long hunter
Maryland-born Henry Skaggs (1724–1810) was among the earliest long hunters through Cumberland Gap—out by 1761 with Elisha Walden, then deeper into the Cumberland and, by 1764, into Kentucky itself. In 1769 he hunted Dix and Green River country with Kasper Mansker, Joseph Drake, and James Knox (the party that found Daniel Boone singing on a deerskin near the Big Barren). Part of the Wilderness Road from Hazel Patch to Crab Orchard still carries his name as Skaggs Trace; in June 1775 he and Valentine Harman guided Col. Thomas Slaughter over Green River land for Henderson’s Transylvania Company. Skaggs Creek in Barren County still marks that range.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Skaggs

🏙️ Springfield, first county seat after statehood
Washington County was the first county the new Kentucky Assembly created (June 1792), carved wholly from Nelson and named for George Washington. Gen. Matthew Walton—Virginia surveyor, Revolutionary officer, and one of the state’s biggest early landholders—introduced the bill, then donated about fifty acres at the county’s surveyed center. Springfield was laid off there in 1793 around a public square; a log courthouse rose early in 1794 (the brick courthouse of 1816 later became Kentucky’s longest-serving). Settlers had already planted Sandusky Station (1776) and Cartwright’s Station (1778) along the Wilderness Trace that still runs through town.
Source: https://history.ky.gov/markers/washington-county

🪶 Odawa raid at Eskippakithiki, 1753
At the Shawnee town of Eskippakithiki (Indian Old Fields, Clark County)—Kentucky’s last major Native town of the 1700s—Pennsylvania trader John Finley kept a store and stockade in 1752. In January 1753 an Odawa (Ottawa) raiding party hit the post: three of Finley’s servants were taken, furs and goods seized; Finley and John Faulkner escaped because they were away that day. The attack, amid rising French–British and intertribal pressure, helped push the Shawnee to abandon the palisaded cornfields by about 1754 and rejoin kin in southern Ohio—clearing the Warrior’s Path years before Boone’s long hunt.
Source: https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/596

🏺 Page Site / “Lost City” (15LO1)
Above the Mud River east of Lewisburg in Logan County sits the Page Site, a major Mississippian mound-and-ossuary complex (roughly A.D. 1000–1600) excavated by William S. Webb and W. D. Funkhouser for the University of Kentucky in the 1930s. For a stretch that same decade the place was run as a heritage tourist stop called Lost City. Listed on the National Register in 1985, it remains one of south-central Kentucky’s clearest windows on Mississippian mortuary practice and settlement along the Green River drainage—far from the Ohio River mound centers that usually get the headlines.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places_listings_in_Logan_County,_Kentucky

🤠 Col. Robert Patterson builds Lexington
In April 1779 Ensign Robert Patterson led about twenty-five men north from Harrodsburg and began a fort on the site that became Lexington, naming the place for the Massachusetts battle that opened the Revolution. He helped lay off the town, sat for years on its board of trustees, and later helped charter Transylvania University. In 1788 he partnered with Matthias Denman and John Filson to found Losantiville (renamed Cincinnati in 1790); he also had a hand in Dayton. His 1780 cabin—moved more than once—stands again on Transylvania’s campus near Third and Broadway.
Source: https://history.ky.gov/markers/patterson-cabin
