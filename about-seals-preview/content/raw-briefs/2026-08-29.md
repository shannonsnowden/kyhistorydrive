Isaac Shelby, "Old Kings Mountain"
Shelby County's namesake was already a frontier celebrity before Kentucky was a state. Maryland-born Isaac Shelby fought at Point Pleasant in Lord Dunmore's War, then helped lead the Overmountain Men to victory at Kings Mountain in 1780, a blow that cracked British control in the South. He moved to Kentucky in the 1780s, helped push statehood, and in 1792 the electoral college unanimously made him the commonwealth's first governor. Sixteen years later he came back as the fifth governor and personally led Kentucky militia at the Battle of the Thames in the War of 1812. The county carved from Jefferson in June 1792 still carries his name.

Frankfort: Frank's Ford becomes the capital
Before it was the capital, the site was a buffalo-trail ford on the Kentucky River. In 1780 pioneer Stephen Frank was killed in a skirmish there; settlers called the crossing Frank's Ford, which shortened to Frankfort. In 1786 Virginia chartered a town on land promoted by Gen. James Wilkinson. When Kentucky became a state in 1792, Frankfort outbid Lexington and others—Andrew Holmes pledged lots, building materials, and cash for a capitol—and the legislature locked it in that December. The first two capitols burned; Gideon Shryock's Greek Revival Old Capitol (1830) finally stuck.

Lower Shawneetown at the Scioto mouth
Long before stations dotted the Bluegrass, a major Shawnee town straddled the Ohio at the Scioto's mouth (near today's South Portsmouth, KY). English maps called it Lower Shawneetown; it may have been a Chillicothe, a principal place of the Chalahgawtha division. By 1751 eyewitnesses put the population around 1,200. It was a diplomatic hub and the western end of Pennsylvania's southern deerskin trade, with English trading houses in town. Between April and November 1758, as the French and Indian War made the river mouth unsafe, the Shawnee moved north. Kentucky archaeology still holds Fort Ancient pottery mixed with mid-1700s European trade goods from the Bentley and related sites on the Kentucky bank.
https://archaeology.ky.gov/Find-a-Site/Pages/Lower-Shawneetown.aspx

Bullitt's Lick: Kentucky's first industry
Salt was frontier "white gold," and the only large commercial saltworks west of the Alleghenies during the Revolution sat about three miles west of present-day Shepherdsville. Capt. Thomas Bullitt surveyed the Salt River lick in 1773 for Col. William Christian; production started in 1779. Workers boiled brine in rows of iron kettles—Col. William Fleming noted a trough holding nearly 2,000 gallons emptied three times a day. Pack trains and flatboats carried the salt to stations across Kentucky and beyond. A fort went up in 1780 after raids, and a boomtown nicknamed Saltburg grew around the furnaces. The fires finally went out around 1830 when cheaper salt arrived from elsewhere.
https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/888

Indian Knoll on the Green River
Five thousand years before the first stations, Late Archaic hunter-gatherers kept returning to a shell midden on the Green River in Ohio County. Indian Knoll (15OH2) yielded over a thousand burials, twenty-three dog burials, and more than 50,000 artifacts—spear points, bone pins, marine shell beads, bannerstones—when University of Kentucky crews dug it in the late 1930s WPA years. People hunted deer, gathered hickory nuts, fished, and harvested mussels from the shoals. In the early 1990s Indian Knoll and nineteen other Green River shell middens became National Historic Landmarks, some of the deepest windows we have on Archaic life in the Ohio Valley.
