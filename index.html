    Kentucky, layer by layer

    The marker map opens on Kentucky Historical Society highway markers. Toggle the other layers, tap a pin to read its story, and stack Native homelands and archaeological cultures under today’s markers to see the deeper history around you, including on the road.

    Archaeological sites are shown at approximate locations. Culture areas are defined by artifacts and sites, not as tribes, and Native areas are general pictures, not boundaries.

    Layers on this map

    • Markers Kentucky Historical Society highway markers.
    • History Places from the daily history briefs, including archaeological sites at approximate locations.
    • Museums Museums and historic houses you can visit.
    • National National Historic Landmarks and National Park Service sites.
    • War Sites Revolutionary War and Civil War places.
    • Good Eats Restaurants, taverns, groceries, and bakeries.
    • Covered Bridges Covered bridges and the streams they cross.
    • Industry Mills, iron furnaces, depots, and other historic works.
    • Newspapers Historic Kentucky newspapers.
    • Parks State parks, resort parks, and state historic sites.
    • Cemeteries Historic cemeteries, from family plots to national cemeteries.
    • Distilleries Kentucky Bourbon Trail distilleries and a few independents.
    • Caves Public show caves. Documented caves without a public entrance are marked at the county center, not the cave.
    • Archaeological cultures (not tribes)
    • Green River Archaic Shell middens in west-central Kentucky, about 4000–1000 BCE.
    • Adena Mounds and earthworks in the Bluegrass and nearby, about 800 BCE–250 CE.
    • Fort Ancient Farming villages in central, northern, and eastern Kentucky, about 1000–1750.
    • Mississippian (western Kentucky) Towns in western and south-central Kentucky, about 1000–1700.
    • Caborn-Welborn Towns near the mouth of the Wabash, about 1400–1700.
    • Native territories
    • Chickasaw Hopewell-frame hunting ground, 1780–1818. Approximate, not a boundary.
    • Cherokee Donelson cession, Lochaber wedge, and Chickamauga counties, 1768–1794.
    • Shawnee Presence in Kentucky and a claim to most of the state, about 1735–1795. A generous estimate, not a boundary.
    • Six Nations (Haudenosaunee) Homeland in New York and Pennsylvania, and a 1768 cession claim east of the Tennessee River.
    • Mingo Upper Ohio and New York presence, about 1750–1832. No Kentucky camps are drawn.
    • Wyandot Towns from Michilimackinac to the Sandusky, 1671–1843. Kentucky appears as 1780 and 1782 raid sites.
    • Delaware (Lenape) Ohio towns and the 1804 Indiana cession, plus Bird’s 1780 route in Kentucky.
    • Miami (with Wea and Piankashaw) Wabash country from Fort Wayne to Vincennes. No Kentucky hunting ground is drawn.
    • Kickapoo (with Mascouten) Wabash and Vermilion country. No Kentucky villages are documented.
    • Illinois Confederation (Kaskaskia, Cahokia) Mississippi towns and an 1803 claim that stops at the Ohio. Nothing in Kentucky is claimed.
    • Potawatomi Great Lakes country, about 1640–1809. No Kentucky presence is drawn.
    • Ojibwe (Chippewa) and Odawa Straits of Mackinac, Detroit, and Saginaw, plus Bird’s 1780 route.
    • Erie Erie and Chautauqua counties in New York, and Erie County, Pennsylvania, about 1600–1656.
    • Susquehannock Lancaster and York counties in Pennsylvania, and a 1675 Maryland county, about 1550–1763.
    • Tutelo Virginia, then Shamokin and Coreorgonel, 1671–1779.
    • Mosopelea (Ofo) Ohio River counties between the Muskingum and the Scioto, then the lower Mississippi, before 1673 to 1784.
    • Osage Osage River villages in Missouri, about 1673–1823. No Kentucky area is drawn.
    • Yuchi Tennessee to 1714, then the Savannah and the Chattahoochee, 1567–1836.

      About

      Official Kentucky History Drive seal

      Kentucky History Drive is a web companion to the KY Markers Drive iPhone app. It helps you explore Kentucky’s historical highway markers on a map, and browse Kentucky history stories on a timeline by era and year.
